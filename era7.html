Era 7

Ming

1368 – 1644

The Ming (明) was the last Chinese dynasty founded by Han Chinese. Much of what visitors today picture as "China" was left by the Ming: the Forbidden City (紫禁城) in Beijing (北京), the Great Wall (長城) built of brick and stone, and blue-and-white porcelain. In the early Ming, Zheng He led huge fleets on seven voyages across the Indian Ocean, nearly a century before Columbus; but the court soon stopped the voyages, and for a long time restricted private overseas trade. In the late Ming, great quantities of silver poured into China from Japan and the Americas, sweet potatoes and maize arrived from the Americas, and European missionaries brought Western science. In the end the Ming fell to natural disasters, popular revolt and attack by the Manchus (滿洲) from the northeast.

Before we begin

Over 270 years, from the Hundred Years' War to the English Civil War

The Ming ruled for 276 years. A European reader can compare it like this: when the Ming was founded, England and France were fighting the Hundred Years' War; in the middle of the Ming, Columbus reached the Americas; in the late Ming, Shakespeare wrote his plays; in 1644, the year the Ming fell, England was in the middle of the civil war between King Charles I and Parliament.

All the Ming emperors had the family name Zhu (朱). From the Ming onwards, each emperor usually used just one (年號, nianhao) for his whole reign, so emperors are often called by their reign titles, for example "the Yongle Emperor" (永樂帝) or "the Wanli Emperor" (萬曆帝). When Western auction houses talk about "Wanli blue-and-white" or a "Xuande censer", they mean objects made during that emperor's reign.

1368 – 1398

Zhu Yuanzhang: from beggar to emperor

The founder of the Ming, (朱元璋), came from an extremely poor farming family. When he was sixteen, his home district was struck by drought, locusts and plague; his parents and elder brother died within a few months, and he could not even afford coffins. He was sent to a temple as a novice monk, spent years begging on the road, then joined the Red Turban rebels (紅巾軍) and through his own ability rose to become a rebel leader. In 1368 he declared himself emperor in Nanjing (南京), founding the Ming with the reign title Hongwu (洪武), and in the same year drove the Mongols (蒙古) back to the northern steppe.

Zhu Yuanzhang knew the hardships of ordinary people well. He ordered lower taxes, new irrigation works and the resettlement of people to farm empty land, and set up detailed registers of households and land, called the (黃冊, huangce) and the fish-scale maps (魚鱗圖冊, yulin tuce), a little like England's Domesday Book of 1086. But he was also an extremely suspicious and cruel ruler. In 1380 he had his chief minister, Hu Weiyong (胡惟庸), executed for treason, and more than thirty thousand people are said to have been put to death as accomplices. After that he simply abolished the post of chief minister, which had existed for more than a thousand years, and handled all government business himself. He also set up the (錦衣衛), a secret police force answering directly to the emperor, which could arrest and interrogate officials without any legal process. Zhu Yuanzhang also strictly banned ordinary people from going to sea to trade with foreign countries, a policy called the (海禁, haijin).

1399 – 1424

An uncle seizes the throne, and the move to Beijing

Zhu Yuanzhang's eldest son died young, so he left the throne to his eldest grandson, the Jianwen Emperor (建文帝). The young Jianwen Emperor worried that his uncles, stationed in different parts of the country, had grown too powerful, and set about cutting their power. His uncle (朱棣), Prince of Yan (燕王), stationed at Beiping (北平, today's Beijing), rose in revolt. After three years of civil war he captured Nanjing in 1402, in what is known as the (靖難之變). The palace caught fire in the chaos and the Jianwen Emperor disappeared: some say he burned to death, while legend says he escaped disguised as a monk. Zhu Di took the throne with the reign title Yongle, becoming the Yongle Emperor.

The Yongle Emperor moved the capital from Nanjing to his own power base, Beijing, and between 1406 and 1420 built the , today's Palace Museum in Beijing. Beijing was close to the northern frontier, so the emperor could take personal charge of the defence against the Mongols; he also had the Grand Canal (大運河) dredged and reopened to carry grain from the south to Beijing. From then until 1912, Beijing remained China's capital. The Yongle Emperor also ordered the compiling of an encyclopedia of unprecedented size, the (永樂大典), in more than eleven thousand volumes, preserving a vast number of ancient books. Sadly the original has been lost, and most of the copy was scattered in later wars; only about four hundred volumes survive today, spread around the world, including in the British Library.

1405 – 1433

Zheng He sails the Western Seas

The Yongle Emperor sent the eunuch (鄭和) on long voyages with huge fleets, known as the (下西洋, xia Xiyang). Zheng He's original family name was Ma (馬); he was a Muslim from Yunnan (雲南), captured as a boy when Ming armies conquered Yunnan, castrated and sent into the household of the Prince of Yan. He later distinguished himself in the Jingnan campaign and won the Yongle Emperor's trust.

Between 1405 and 1433 Zheng He made seven voyages, passing through Southeast Asia, India and Sri Lanka, and reaching the Persian Gulf, the Arabian peninsula and the coast of East Africa (see Map 1). The first voyage had more than two hundred ships of all sizes and over twenty-seven thousand men; the largest ships were called (寶船, baochuan). By comparison, when the Portuguese Vasco da Gama sailed round Africa to India more than ninety years later, he had just four ships and about 170 men (see Figure 2). The fleets carried large amounts of silk, porcelain and copper coins, spread the prestige of the Ming, invited other countries to send envoys with tribute, and traded with places along the way. One East African state sent a giraffe, which the Chinese took to be the qilin (麒麟), a legendary creature of good fortune, causing a great sensation.

Zheng He's voyages were not meant to found colonies or find new trade routes, but to show off the empire's power and set up (朝貢, chaogong) relationships; some think they were also meant to search for the missing Jianwen Emperor. The voyages were enormously expensive, and after the Yongle Emperor's death the court decided they were not worth it: after 1433 no more fleets were sent. One story says later officials burned Zheng He's voyage records, but this is not certain. At about the same time, the Portuguese were beginning to explore southwards down the west coast of Africa. Did China miss the age of the oceans from then on? It is a question Chinese people often discuss today.

1449 – 1600

The threat from the north: Tumu and the Great Wall

The Mongols, driven back to the steppe, were still the Ming's greatest threat. In 1449 the young Zhengtong Emperor (正統帝), listening to the eunuch Wang Zhen (王振), personally led an army of several hundred thousand against the Oirat Mongols (瓦剌). At Tumu (土木堡), northwest of Beijing, the army was surrounded and routed, and the emperor himself was captured: the (土木堡之變). The Mongol army then advanced to the walls of Beijing. The vice minister of war (兵部侍郎), (于謙), overruled those who wanted to move the capital and flee south, put a new emperor on the throne, and organised soldiers and citizens to hold Beijing.

After this the Ming turned mainly to defence, and built the on a huge scale. From the late fifteenth century to the early seventeenth, the Ming rebuilt the old rammed-earth walls as solid walls of brick and stone, with beacon towers and fortified passes, from Jiayuguan (嘉峪關) in Gansu (甘肅) in the west to Shanhaiguan (山海關) on the Bohai Sea (渤海) in the east (see Map 3). Most of the magnificent Great Wall that visitors see today at Badaling (八達嶺) and Mutianyu (慕田峪) was built in the late Ming, more than 1,700 years after the First Emperor of Qin.

1540 – 1567

Pirates in the south, and Macau

The sea ban did not stop trade by sea; it just turned it into smuggling. In the mid-sixteenth century, large bands of armed pirates appeared on the southeast coast, raiding towns. They were called (倭寇). "Wo" (倭) was the name then used for Japan, but most of these pirates were actually Chinese coastal smugglers and fishermen who had lost their living; real Japanese were only a minority. The famous general (戚繼光) recruited farmers and miners in Zhejiang (浙江), trained them strictly into the "Qi Family Army" (戚家軍), and defeated the pirates again and again. The Ming also finally understood that the army alone could not solve the problem: in 1567 the court opened the port of Yuegang (月港) in Fujian (福建), allowing private merchants to sail abroad to trade, and the pirate problem quickly shrank.

In the same period, Europeans arrived by sea. In 1513 the Portuguese reached the coast of southern China by ship for the first time, right in what is now the Hong Kong (香港) area (see "Hong Kong"). After clashes and negotiations, in about 1557 the Portuguese were allowed to live and trade on a small peninsula in southern Guangdong (廣東): this was (澳門). Macau was run by the Portuguese for more than four hundred years, and was only returned to China in 1999.

1550 – 1644

Silver, sweet potatoes and blue-and-white porcelain

In the late Ming, China was drawn into a global trade network unlike anything before. The Spanish found a huge silver mine at Potosí in South America. In 1571 they took Manila in the Philippines, and every year sent great galleons across the Pacific, carrying American silver to Manila to exchange for silk and porcelain brought by Chinese merchants (see Map 4). Japan was also producing a lot of silver at the time. The Chinese valued silver highly but had little demand for foreign goods, so some scholars estimate that a large share of all the silver mined in the world in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries ended up in China.

All this silver made the Ming economy heavily based on money. In 1581 the senior grand secretary (首輔, shoufu), (張居正), brought in the (一條鞭法, yitiaobianfa) across the whole country, merging the various land taxes and labour duties and requiring them all to be paid in silver. This was a major reform of the Chinese tax system, but it also left Ming finances at the mercy of the silver supply.

Along with the silver came crops from the Americas: sweet potatoes, maize and peanuts, and a little later chilli peppers and tobacco. Sweet potatoes and maize could be grown on hillsides and sandy land unsuited to rice and wheat, and helped China's population grow greatly over the next two hundred years. The chillies in today's Sichuan (四川) and Hunan (湖南) cooking actually only arrived from the Americas at the very end of the Ming.

Going the other way, the most important thing to leave China was porcelain. The (青花瓷, qinghuaci) made at (景德鎮) in Jiangxi (江西), painted in blue on white with cobalt imported from Persia, was exported in great quantities to Southeast Asia, the Middle East and Europe. Europeans prized Chinese porcelain so highly that in English it is simply called "china". The Dutch copied Chinese blue-and-white and developed Delftware.

1500 – 1620

Ideas, novels and medicine

In the mid-Ming the thinker (王陽明) challenged the Neo-Confucianism (理學, lixue) of Zhu Xi (朱熹). He argued that moral truth did not have to be sought in books and outside things: everyone already had in their own heart an "innate knowledge" (良知) of good and evil; once you know what is right you must act on it, because knowing and doing are one, an idea called "the unity of knowledge and action" (知行合一). His teaching is called the (心學, xinxue), and it later became influential in Japan too.

As printing and cities flourished, popular fiction became a huge success. Three of China's four great classical novels (四大古典小說), Romance of the Three Kingdoms (三國演義), The Water Margin (水滸傳) and Journey to the West (西遊記), were written or took their final form in the Ming. The playwright (湯顯祖) wrote the love story The Peony Pavilion (牡丹亭), which is still performed today; he and Shakespeare both died in 1616. The physician (李時珍) spent nearly thirty years travelling to collect medicinal plants and wrote a great work on materia medica, the (本草綱目, Bencao Gangmu), recording nearly 1,900 medicines.

1582 – 1610

Matteo Ricci: a missionary in scholar's robes

In 1582 the Italian Jesuit missionary (利瑪竇) arrived in Macau and soon entered mainland China. He found that the people the Chinese respected most were scholars, so he learned Chinese, studied the Confucian classics closely, put on the long robe of a Confucian scholar, and made friends with the educated officials as a scholar himself. In 1601 he was allowed to live in Beijing, where he stayed until his death.

Ricci did not preach directly. Instead he first introduced Western astronomy, mathematics and geography. His (坤輿萬國全圖) was the first world map in China to show the Americas and the other continents, and he deliberately put China in the middle of the map. Working with the scholar (徐光啟), he translated the first six books of the ancient Greek Euclid's Elements into Chinese; the Chinese word for geometry they used, jihe (幾何), is still in use today. Ricci allowed Chinese converts to go on honouring their ancestors and Confucius, believing these were rituals of respect rather than religious worship, but this practice later sparked a dispute within the Catholic Church that lasted a hundred years.

1572 – 1627

Wanli: the emperor who stopped holding court

The Wanli Emperor came to the throne before he was ten. For the first ten years the senior grand secretary Zhang Juzheng ran the government and pushed through reforms, and the treasury filled up. After Zhang Juzheng died, the Wanli Emperor, who resented the strict way Zhang had treated him, ordered his family's property confiscated. Later Wanli quarrelled with his ministers over the choice of crown prince, and simply stopped holding court for years on end: for nearly thirty years he rarely met his ministers, and many official posts were left empty.

In 1592 Japan's ruler, (豐臣秀吉), sent a large army to invade Korea, intending to pass through it to conquer China. The Ming sent troops to help Korea and fought the Japanese hard for seven years, until the Japanese withdrew after Hideyoshi's death. This is known as the (萬曆朝鮮之役). This war and other frontier wars drained Ming finances. After Wanli, the eunuch (魏忠賢) took control at court and persecuted a group of upright officials known as the (東林黨), and government grew steadily more rotten.

1616 – 1644

The fall of the Ming

In the first half of the seventeenth century the world entered an especially cold period, called the . North China suffered severe drought year after year, followed by locusts, famine and epidemics, and countless people starved. To pay for its wars, the court kept raising taxes, and to save money it cut staff at the government courier stations. A laid-off courier, (李自成), and a former soldier, Zhang Xianzhong (張獻忠), among others, led starving people in revolts that grew ever larger.

Meanwhile, in the northeast, the Jurchen (女真) were united under (努爾哈赤), who in 1616 founded the "Later Jin" (後金). In 1636 his son Hong Taiji (皇太極) changed the state's name to "Qing" (清) and the people's name to "Manchu" (滿洲). The Ming's best troops were tied down in the northeast around Shanhaiguan and could not put their full strength into crushing the revolts.

In April 1644 Li Zicheng's army captured Beijing. The last Ming emperor, (崇禎), hanged himself on Coal Hill (煤山) behind the Forbidden City, and the Ming dynasty was over. The Ming general then guarding Shanhaiguan, (吳三桂), refused to surrender to Li Zicheng; instead he opened the pass at Shanhaiguan and joined the Qing army to defeat Li Zicheng. The Qing army then entered Beijing, and went on to rule China for more than 260 years (see Era 8).

Daily life

How people lived

The market towns of Jiangnan

In the late Ming, the Jiangnan (江南) region on the lower Yangtze (長江) saw many market towns grow up around spinning, weaving, silk and trade. Many farming families no longer grew only food, but grew cotton or raised silkworms, while women spun yarn and wove cloth at home to sell to merchants for silver. Suzhou (蘇州), Hangzhou (杭州) and Nanjing were the richest and most fashionable cities in the country, and the wealthy built elaborate private gardens; Suzhou's Humble Administrator's Garden (拙政園) was built at this time.

Study and examinations

The Ming civil service examination (科舉, keju) required candidates to write essays in a fixed format, called the (八股文, baguwen), with every topic taken from the Four Books (四書) as explained by Zhu Xi. Candidates memorised the classics from childhood and worked their way up level by level, and most never passed in their whole lives. Those who passed brought glory to their families; those who failed could become private tutors, officials' secretaries or merchants.

Family and women

The Ming placed special stress on female chastity. Widows who never remarried, or even took their own lives after their husbands died, could be honoured by the court, and a great many "chastity arches" (貞節牌坊) were built across the country. Foot-binding (纏足) spread to more levels of society at this time. Yet many women could read and write, and the upper-class families of Jiangnan produced quite a few women poets and painters.

Key people

Who's who

  • Zhu Yuanzhang (朱元璋), the Hongwu EmperorFounder of the Ming, 1328–1398

    Rose from beggar and monk to emperor. He built a highly centralised system of rule, slaughtered the men who had helped him to power and strictly banned overseas trade.

  • Zhu Di (朱棣), the Yongle EmperorEmperor, 1360–1424

    Seized the throne from his nephew, moved the capital to Beijing, built the Forbidden City, sent Zheng He to the Western Seas and had the Yongle Encyclopedia compiled.

  • Zheng He (鄭和)Navigator, 1371–1433

    A eunuch from a Muslim family in Yunnan who led huge fleets on seven voyages across the Indian Ocean, reaching as far as East Africa.

  • Wang Yangming (王陽明, Wang Shouren)Thinker and general, 1472–1529

    Taught that "the mind is principle" and "the unity of knowledge and action", founding the School of Mind; he was also a capable official who put down rebellions.

  • Zhang Juzheng (張居正)Senior grand secretary, 1525–1582

    Led reforms in the early Wanli years and brought in the Single Whip reform, filling the treasury; after his death his family's property was confiscated.

  • Matteo Ricci (利瑪竇)Italian missionary, 1552–1610

    Spread Christianity in China in the guise of a Confucian scholar and introduced Western science. A pioneer of cultural exchange between East and West, he is buried in Beijing.

  • Wu Sangui (吳三桂)Ming general, 1612–1678

    Guarded Shanhaiguan, and in 1644 let the Qing army through the pass to defeat Li Zicheng. Under the Qing he later became a feudatory prince in Yunnan, and in 1673 rose against the Qing (see Era 8).

Myth or fact

What people often get wrong

  • "Zheng He discovered America before Columbus."No evidence. This claim comes from 1421, a 2002 bestseller by the British author Gavin Menzies, but historians generally agree it lacks reliable evidence. Zheng He's fleets got no further than East Africa.
  • "Ming China was always closed to the outside world."Not quite. The early Ming sea ban was strict, but Yuegang in Fujian was opened in 1567, private overseas trade boomed, and silver from the Americas and Japan poured in. Official restrictions and real trade were not the same thing.
  • "The Great Wall kept out the northern peoples."Only up to a point. The Wall could stop small-scale raids, but not large armies. The Mongols broke through it many times, and in the end the Qing army only got through Shanhaiguan because its commander, Wu Sangui, opened the gate.
  • "The Forbidden City has 9,999 and a half rooms."A legend. A survey carried out between 2002 and 2005 counted about 8,700 rooms in the Forbidden City.
How we know

Veritable Records, inscriptions and foreigners' accounts

Official records

After each Ming emperor died, the court compiled the Veritable Records (實錄) of his reign from the archives. The History of Ming (明史), compiled under the Qing, is the official dynastic history. Locally, the Ming produced a great many local gazetteers recording each county's geography, population, products and notable people; the Yue Da Ji (粵大記), a record of Guangdong, is one of them.

Zheng He's footprints

Most of Zheng He's voyage records are lost, but his interpreter Ma Huan (馬歡) and others who sailed with him wrote travel accounts describing the countries along the way. Zheng He left a stone tablet in Sri Lanka inscribed in Chinese, Tamil and Persian, which is now in the National Museum in Colombo.

Missionaries and merchants

Matteo Ricci's journals were edited by a fellow Jesuit and published in Europe, giving Europeans their first fairly detailed picture of Ming China. The letters and reports of Portuguese and Spanish merchants record the trade and conflicts along the coast.

Key terms

Terms from this era

Reign title (年號)
The name an emperor gave to the years of his reign. Ming and Qing emperors usually used just one in their lifetime, so emperors are often known by it.
Sea ban (海禁)
The policy banning ordinary people from going to sea to trade with foreign countries.
Tribute (朝貢)
A system of diplomacy and trade in which foreign rulers sent envoys with gifts to the Chinese emperor, who gave gifts in return.
Wokou (倭寇)
Armed pirates on the southeast coast in the Ming; in the sixteenth century most were actually Chinese.
Single Whip reform (一條鞭法)
Zhang Juzheng's tax reform, merging the various taxes and labour duties into one, paid in silver.
Blue-and-white porcelain (青花瓷)
White porcelain painted with blue designs in cobalt, made at Jingdezhen and sold all over the world.
Eight-legged essay (八股文)
The fixed essay format required in the Ming and Qing civil service examinations.
School of Mind (心學)
Wang Yangming's teaching, stressing the innate knowledge in everyone's heart and the unity of knowledge and action.
Hong Kong

Hong Kong in the Ming

In 1513 the Portuguese Jorge Álvares (歐維士) arrived by ship in the Tuen Mun (屯門) area and set up a stone pillar carved with the Portuguese royal arms. The Portuguese were the first Europeans to reach China by sea. They went on to set up a base around Tuen Mun, building forts without permission and forcing trade. In 1521 the Ming navy in Guangdong drove them out, in what is known as the Battle of Tunmen (屯門海戰), the first armed clash between China and a European country.

In 1573 the Ming split off the southern part of Dongguan (東莞) county to create Xin'an county (新安縣), which governed what are now both Hong Kong and Shenzhen (深圳). The name "Hong Kong" (香港, "fragrant harbour") first appears on a coastal defence map attached to the Yue Da Ji, compiled in the Wanli reign, where it referred to a small village or a bay on the south side of Hong Kong Island. A popular explanation is that the area was then a port for exporting incense such as guanxiang (莞香) from Dongguan, hence the name "fragrant harbour", but this has not been settled.

Meanwhile in Europe and the world

The Age of Discovery

Two kinds of voyage

More than fifty years after Zheng He's last voyage ended, Columbus reached the Americas in 1492, and in 1498 da Gama reached Calicut in India, where Zheng He's fleets had stopped many times. Their aims were completely different: Zheng He's was to show off the empire's power and set up tribute relationships, while the Europeans were looking for spices, gold and colonies. European voyages kept expanding, while the Ming voyages stopped.

Renaissance and Reformation

In the mid-Ming, Italy was in the Renaissance, with Leonardo da Vinci and Michelangelo at work; in 1517 Martin Luther began the Reformation in Germany. In 1540 the Catholic Church founded the Jesuits, who became a leading force in Catholic reform and missions overseas; Matteo Ricci was a Jesuit missionary.

Mary, Queen of Scots and the Union of the Crowns

In the Jiajing reign, Mary, Queen of Scots inherited the throne a few days after her birth in 1542, and was later executed by Elizabeth I of England. In the thirty-first year of Wanli, 1603, her son, King James VI of Scotland, inherited the English throne and became monarch of both countries, in what is known as the Union of the Crowns.

See it for yourself

Places to visit

  • The Palace Museum (故宮博物院; Forbidden City, 紫禁城)Beijing

    A World Heritage Site and the largest surviving group of wooden palace buildings in the world, where twenty-four Ming and Qing emperors lived and governed.

  • Ming Tombs (明十三陵)Changping, Beijing

    A World Heritage Site: the tombs of thirteen Ming emperors. The Wanli Emperor's tomb, Dingling, was excavated in 1956, and you can go down into its underground palace.

  • Great Wall at Badaling (八達嶺) or Mutianyu (慕田峪)Yanqing and Huairou, Beijing

    The two most famous stretches of the brick-and-stone wall built by the Ming. Mutianyu is less crowded.

  • Nanjing City Wall (南京城牆) and Ming Xiaoling Mausoleum (明孝陵)Nanjing, Jiangsu

    The city wall Zhu Yuanzhang built at Nanjing was originally about 35 km long, one of the longest surviving ancient city walls in the world; the Ming Xiaoling Mausoleum is his tomb.

  • Historic Centre of Macau (澳門歷史城區)Macau

    A World Heritage Site, with the Ruins of St Paul's, A-Ma Temple and the churches, forts and squares left by the Portuguese, showing more than four hundred years of exchange between China and the West.

  • Castle Peak Bay (青山灣) and Pui To Rock (杯渡岩), Tuen MunTuen Mun, Hong Kong

    The area of the Battle of Tunmen, where the Portuguese first arrived; the exact location is still debated.

  • British Museum and Victoria and Albert MuseumLondon, UK · free

    The British Museum's Sir Percival David Collection and the V&A's China gallery both have fine Ming blue-and-white porcelain and cloisonné.

The one thing to rememberThe Ming was the last Han Chinese dynasty. It built the Forbidden City and the brick-and-stone Great Wall, and sent Zheng He across the Indian Ocean before turning inwards; yet in the late Ming, silver, crops from the Americas and missionaries still drew China into the global world that was taking shape.

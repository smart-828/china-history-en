A few things to know before reading Chinese history

Geography · Names · Dynasties

This page does not tell a story. Instead it explains a few things that come up again and again in Chinese history but are seldom explained: how big China is, how its mountains and rivers shaped its history, why the capital kept moving, how Chinese names work, why emperors had so many names, and what a "dynasty" actually is. Ten minutes spent here will make the ten eras that follow much easier to understand.

How big is China?

China today covers about 9.6 million square kilometres, roughly the size of the whole of Europe and about forty times the size of the United Kingdom (see Map 1). If you laid a map of China over Europe, it would stretch from the Atlantic to beyond Moscow. A single Chinese province, Sichuan (四川), is about as big as France. From Beijing (北京) to Urumqi (烏魯木齊) in the northwest is about 2,400 km, much the same as London to Moscow; yet the whole of China uses one time zone, Beijing time.

China's population today is about 1.4 billion, more than twice that of all of Europe (a little over 700 million). For most of history, China has been home to between a quarter and a third of all the people in the world.

Bear in mind, though, that China in the past was not always this big. Today's borders took shape roughly after the Qing (清) dynasty's expansion in the eighteenth century. Under the Han (漢) and Tang (唐) dynasties, the heartland of each dynasty was mainly the valleys of the Yellow River (黃河) and the Yangtze (長江) in the east. What are now Tibet (西藏), Xinjiang (新疆), Inner Mongolia (內蒙古) and the Northeast (東北) were for long periods the homelands of other peoples: sometimes they belonged to the Chinese dynasties of the central plains, and sometimes they were independent states. So whenever you read "China", ask yourself which lands it meant at the time.

2

Two rivers, two lines

China's land is high in the west and low in the east, like a staircase with three steps. Furthest west is the Tibetan Plateau (青藏高原), on average more than 4,000 metres above sea level, with the world's highest mountain, Everest (珠穆朗瑪峰, Qomolangma), on its southern edge. In the middle are plateaus and basins, and in the east are broad plains. That is why most of China's great rivers flow from west to east, into the Pacific (see Map 2).

The two most important rivers are the Yellow River (黃河) in the north and the Yangtze (長江) in the south. The Yellow River flows through the soft, crumbly soil of the Loess Plateau (黃土高原) and carries so much silt that its water is yellow, which is how it got its name. The silt settles and keeps raising the riverbed downstream, so in the course of history the Yellow River has often burst its banks and changed course, causing terrible floods. Westerners called it "China's Sorrow" (中國之患, Zhongguo zhi huan); but it was also the cradle of the earliest civilisation of the central plains. The Yangtze is about 6,300 km long, the third-longest river in the world. Its valley is warm and wet, and it later became the richest part of China.

The map also has two lines you cannot see but which matter a great deal:

  • The north–south divide (the line of the Qinling (秦嶺) mountains and the Huai River (淮河)): north of this line, winters are cold and dry; people traditionally grew wheat and millet and ate noodles and bread. South of it is warm and humid; people grow rice and eat rice, there are many rivers and lakes, and people got about by boat. Even today Chinese people often speak of "southerners" and "northerners", who differ in accent, food and habits.
  • The line between farming and herding (roughly the line of 400 mm of rain a year): southeast of this line there is enough rain to farm, and it belonged to farming peoples. Northwest of it is too dry for anything but grazing animals: grassland and desert, home to nomads on horseback. Much of Chinese history is the story of these two kinds of people: the farming dynasties of the central plains and the peoples of the northern steppe, such as the Xiongnu (匈奴), the Turks (突厥) and the Mongols (蒙古), who sometimes traded, sometimes intermarried and sometimes went to war. The Great Wall (萬里長城) was built roughly along this line.

China is surrounded by natural barriers: the sea to the east, the Himalayas, the world's highest mountains, and the Tibetan Plateau to the southwest, deserts and the Gobi to the northwest, and mountains and jungle to the south. For a very long time this kept China fairly cut off from the other great civilisations, such as India, Persia and the Mediterranean world. Foreign people and goods came in mainly by two routes: the Silk Road (絲綢之路) in the northwest, and the sea routes along the southeast coast.

Where was the capital?

Down the ages China had six main capitals (see Map 3). The broad pattern is this: early capitals were in the west, in the valleys of the Wei (渭河) and the Yellow River (Xi'an (西安) and Luoyang (洛陽)), because that was the first centre of farming and was protected by mountains and rivers. Later the centre of the economy gradually moved east and south, and the capital followed it east to Kaifeng (開封) and south to Hangzhou (杭州) and Nanjing (南京). Finally, because of threats from the north and the need to rule the steppe, the capital settled in Beijing, close to the frontier.

The same city often had different names in different periods. For example, Xi'an (西安) was once called Chang'an (長安); Beijing (北京) was once Dadu (大都) and Beiping (北平); Hangzhou (杭州) was once Lin'an (臨安); and Nanjing (南京) was once Jiankang (建康). This site gives the modern name the first time a city appears.

How do Chinese names work?

In Chinese names the family name comes first and the given name second, the opposite of English. Take Mao Zedong (毛澤東): "Mao" is his family name and "Zedong" his given name, so in English he is Mr Mao, not Mr Zedong (see Figure 4). A family name usually has just one character; the most common are Wang (王), Li (李) and Zhang (張), each shared by about 100 million people. A given name usually has one or two characters. Traditionally, women did not change their family name when they married.

This English version of the site spells Chinese names in pinyin (拼音), the standard spelling system China began using in 1958, which is now used internationally. Older English books sometimes use a different system, so the same person or place may look different in English.

Emperors' names are the most complicated, because one emperor often had several names:

  • Personal name: the name he was born with. For example, Emperor Taizong of Tang (唐太宗) was born Li Shimin (李世民). Once he became emperor, his subjects were not allowed to say or write his personal name.
  • Reign title: a name the emperor chose for counting the years, for example Zhenguan (貞觀). Years were counted by reign title plus a number: "the third year of Zhenguan" is AD 629.
  • Temple name: the title used for the emperor in the ancestral temple after his death, for example Taizu (太祖, "Grand Founder", for the first emperor of a dynasty) or Taizong (太宗).
  • Posthumous name: a title given after death that judged his life, for example Wu (武), "martial", for great military success, while Yang (煬) was a term of disapproval.

By custom, emperors up to the Han dynasty are mostly known by their posthumous names (such as Emperor Wu of Han (漢武帝)), those from the Tang to the Yuan (元) mostly by their temple names (such as Emperor Taizong of Tang and Emperor Huizong of Song (宋徽宗)). Ming (明) and Qing emperors usually kept a single reign title for their whole reign, so they are mostly known by it (such as the Yongle Emperor (永樂帝) and the Kangxi Emperor (康熙帝)). This site uses the most common form of each name and explains it the first time it appears.

What is a "dynasty"?

A dynasty (朝代) is a line of rulers from the same family, handing power down from generation to generation. When a family won control of the country, it gave the state a name, and that became the name of the dynasty. Early dynasty names mostly came from the founder's original fief or noble title, for example Qin (秦), Han, Tang and Song (宋). From the Yuan dynasty onwards, names were chosen for their good meanings, such as Yuan (元, "great", "origin"), Ming (明, "bright") and Qing (清, "clear").

Traditional Chinese historians believed dynasties rose and fell in a cycle. A virtuous founder received the "Mandate of Heaven" (天命); later rulers became corrupt and lost their virtue, disasters natural and man-made followed one after another, the people rose in revolt, and the Mandate passed to a new family (see Era 2). History also swung back and forth between unity and division: after a great united empire collapsed there was often a period of division, until a new dynasty reunited the country.

China's main dynasties, in order, are:

  • Xia (夏), Shang (商), Zhou (周): the "Three Dynasties" (三代, sandai) of ancient times (Eras 1 to 3).
  • Qin, Han: the first united empire (Era 4).
  • Three Kingdoms (三國), Jin (晉), Northern and Southern Dynasties (南北朝), Sui (隋), Tang: division, then reunification (Era 5).
  • Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms (五代十國), Song, Yuan: the Song alongside northern peoples, until the Mongols united the whole (Era 6).
  • Ming, Qing: the last two dynasties (Eras 7 and 8).
  • Republic of China (中華民國), People's Republic of China (中華人民共和國): the republics after 1912 (Eras 9 and 10).

The home page has a chart of the dynasties drawn to scale, showing how long each one lasted and which periods were united and which divided.

A few common words

  • BC and AD: Chinese has its own words for these (公元前 and 公元), but the years are counted the same way as in the West.
  • "Zhongguo" (中國): the Chinese name for China. It originally meant "the central lands" and first referred to the middle reaches of the Yellow River; only later did it become the name of the whole country. The English word "China" may come from the sound of "Qin" (秦), the first dynasty to unite the country.
  • The Han Chinese and other peoples: the Han (漢族) are China's largest ethnic group, named after the Han dynasty, and today make up about nine-tenths of China's population. China officially recognises fifty-six ethnic groups, including the Manchu (滿), Mongols, Tibetans (藏), Uyghurs (維吾爾) and Zhuang (壯).
  • Spoken and written Chinese: the spoken dialects of China differ greatly. Mandarin as spoken in Beijing and Cantonese (粵語) as spoken in Hong Kong (香港), for example, are not mutually intelligible, but they are written with essentially the same characters. In the 1950s mainland China brought in simplified characters with fewer strokes, while Hong Kong and Taiwan (台灣) kept the traditional characters. Where this site gives Chinese characters, they are traditional characters, as used in Hong Kong and Taiwan.

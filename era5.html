Era 5

Division, Sui and Tang

AD 220 – 907

After the Han dynasty (漢朝) fell, China was divided for more than three hundred years. First came the Three Kingdoms (三國); later, nomadic peoples from the north moved south one after another and set up their own states, while the Han Chinese court retreated south of the Yangtze (長江), leaving north and south facing each other. In the same period the Western Roman Empire also collapsed, but Europe was never reunited, whereas China was reunited by the Sui dynasty (隋朝) in AD 589. The Tang dynasty (唐朝) that followed was one of the most open and international periods in Chinese history: its capital, Chang'an (長安), was home to people from Central Asia, Persia, Japan and Korea; Buddhism, which had come from India, took root in China; and Tang poetry (唐詩) became the high point of Chinese literature. In the end, a rebellion turned the Tang from glory to decline.

Before we start

Nearly seven hundred years, in three stages

This era is long, with a great many dynasty names, and it is the easiest one to get confused by. It can be simplified into three stages (see Figure 2):

  • Division (220–589): first the Three Kingdoms, then a brief reunification under the Jin dynasty (晉朝); after that the north was ruled by a succession of states founded by different peoples, and the south by a succession of Han Chinese courts. This is called the (南北朝).
  • Sui (581–618): a short-lived dynasty that reunited China and dug the Grand Canal (大運河).
  • Tang (618–907): a great unified dynasty lasting nearly three hundred years, strong and open in its first half, gradually declining in its second.

European readers can line it up like this: the Han dynasty was roughly contemporary with the Roman Empire; China's three-plus centuries of division were the age when the Western Roman Empire declined and fell and Germanic peoples moved in; and the Tang was contemporary with the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms of England, Charlemagne's Frankish empire and the rise of the Islamic empire. A question worth thinking about: why was China eventually reunited, when Europe was not?

AD 208 – 280

The Three Kingdoms: China's most famous age of chaos

At the end of the Eastern Han (東漢), warlords carved up the country until three powers were left: (曹操) in the north, (劉備) in Sichuan (四川) in the southwest, and (孫權) on the lower Yangtze. In AD 208 Cao Cao led a huge army south, hoping to unify the realm at a stroke, but at (赤壁), on the Yangtze, he was defeated by the allied forces of Sun Quan and Liu Bei, who attacked with fire. After this the three sides stood like the three legs of a tripod. In AD 220 Cao Pi (曹丕) deposed the Han emperor and made himself emperor of a new state, Wei (魏); Liu Bei and Sun Quan also declared themselves emperors in turn, founding Shu Han (蜀漢) and Wu (吳). Historians call this the (see Map 1).

Liu Bei's chief minister, (諸葛亮), is for Chinese people the very picture of wisdom and loyalty. After Liu Bei died, he served Liu Bei's son, led several northern campaigns against Wei, and finally died of illness while with the army.

The Three Kingdoms lasted only about sixty years, yet it is the period of history Chinese people know best, mainly because of a Ming dynasty (明朝) novel written more than a thousand years later, the (三國演義). The novel mixes real history with a great deal of invented story: Liu Bei is written as a benevolent ruler and Cao Cao as a cunning villain. The Three Kingdoms are well known in the West today too, for example through the video games Total War: Three Kingdoms and Dynasty Warriors, and John Woo's film Red Cliff. In the end, the Sima (司馬) family, the powerful ministers of Wei, seized the throne and founded the , which unified China in AD 280.

AD 291 – 420

The northern peoples move south, and the elite flee south

The Jin unification was very short-lived. Eight princes of the Jin royal family fought each other for power for more than ten years, in what is known as the (八王之亂), and the state was badly weakened. These princes also recruited nomadic peoples from the north as soldiers. In AD 311 an army of the Xiongnu (匈奴) captured Luoyang (洛陽) and took the Jin emperor prisoner; a few years later Chang'an fell as well, and Jin rule in the north was over.

For more than a century after this, the north was ruled in turn by regimes of many different peoples, traditionally known together as the and Sixteen Kingdoms (五胡十六國). "Hu" (胡) was the general word the Han Chinese of the time used for foreign peoples to the north and west. Large numbers of northern aristocrats, scholars and ordinary people fled south of the Yangtze, and the Jin royal family rebuilt its court at Jiankang (建康, today's Nanjing); this is called the (東晉). This great migration is known as the (衣冠南渡, yiguan nandu). The south had been thinly populated, and the northern migrants brought labour and skills; from then on the Yangtze valley gradually developed, and in the end overtook the north economically.

In AD 383 Fu Jian (苻堅), emperor of the Former Qin (前秦), who had unified the north, led an army said to number nearly a million south to destroy the Eastern Jin, but at the Fei River (淝水) he was routed by a much smaller Jin army. It is said that his fleeing soldiers took the sound of the wind and the cries of cranes for pursuing troops, and mistook the grass and trees on the hills for enemy soldiers. The Chinese sayings "wind's sound and crane's cry" (風聲鶴唳) and "every tree and blade of grass a soldier" (草木皆兵), both used for jumpy panic, come from this (淝水之戰). The battle saved the Han Chinese state in the south.

AD 386 – 589

The Northern and Southern Dynasties: two different worlds

In AD 439 the (北魏), founded by the Xianbei (鮮卑) people, unified the north. The Xianbei were originally nomads from the northeast. Once they ruled the heartland of China and faced far larger numbers of Han Chinese, they had to decide whether to keep their own traditions or adopt Han Chinese institutions. (孝文帝) of the Northern Wei chose thorough sinicisation: in AD 494 he moved the capital from Pingcheng (平城, today's Datong (大同) in Shanxi (山西)) in the north to Luoyang, and ordered that only Chinese be spoken at court. Xianbei nobles had to wear Han Chinese dress and take Han Chinese surnames; even the royal family changed its own surname from "Tuoba" (拓跋) to "Yuan" (元). He also encouraged marriage between Xianbei and Han Chinese. These reforms sped up the mixing of peoples, but they also angered the Xianbei soldiers left on the northern frontier, and the Northern Wei later split apart. In fact, many Chinese people today have Xianbei and other northern peoples among their ancestors.

The south, meanwhile, went through four dynasties in turn, Song, Qi, Liang and Chen (宋、齊、梁、陳), all with their capital at Jiankang, known together as the (南朝). Southern politics was dominated by a few great families, illustrious for generations, who cared about pedigree, refined conversation and elegance. This was an important period for Chinese calligraphy, poetry and the appreciation of landscape: (王羲之) of the Eastern Jin is revered as the "Sage of Calligraphy" (書聖), and the poet (陶淵明) gave up office to live as a farmer, writing The Peach Blossom Spring (桃花源記), a description of an ideal world.

AD 366 – 589

Buddhism in an age of chaos

Buddhism had already reached China under the Eastern Han, but it only truly flourished in the war-torn Northern and Southern Dynasties. Buddhism taught that life is suffering but that one can be freed from it through religious practice, and it had ideas of karma and rebirth, which brought comfort to people living through chaotic times. The non-Han rulers of the north were also happy to support a religion that did not come from the Han Chinese tradition.

Northern rulers had huge cave temples cut into cliffs, filled with carved and painted Buddhas: work on the (莫高窟) at Dunhuang (敦煌) in Gansu (甘肅) began in AD 366; the Northern Wei cut the (雲岡石窟) near Pingcheng, and after moving the capital, the (龍門石窟) near Luoyang. All three are World Heritage Sites today. In the south, Emperor Wu of Liang (梁武帝) was a devout Buddhist who four times "gave himself" to a monastery as a monk, so that his ministers had to buy him back with huge sums. The Tang poet Du Mu (杜牧) wrote of "the Southern Dynasties' four hundred and eighty temples" to describe how many there were.

Chinese monks also travelled to India in search of scriptures. The Eastern Jin monk (法顯) set out from Chang'an in AD 399, was already in his sixties when he reached India, and came home by sea by way of Sri Lanka. The journey took fourteen years in all, more than two hundred years before the more famous Xuanzang (玄奘).

AD 581 – 618

Sui: a short-lived but important reunification

In AD 581 (楊堅), a minister in the north, seized power and founded the Sui dynasty, becoming Emperor Wen of Sui (隋文帝). In AD 589 Sui armies crossed the Yangtze and destroyed the southern Chen dynasty, and China, divided for nearly three hundred years, was united again. Emperor Wen was frugal and hard-working, put the institutions of government in order, and the state quickly became rich and strong.

His son, (隋煬帝), was even more ambitious. He used millions of labourers to dig a linking north and south (see Map 3). China's main rivers all flow from west to east, and there was no waterway connecting north and south; yet by now the south had become an important grain-growing region, while the political and military centre was in the north. The Grand Canal, centred on Luoyang, ran south to Hangzhou (杭州) and north to near today's Beijing (北京), more than two thousand kilometres in all, carrying grain and goods from the south to the north. Parts of it are still in use today, and it is a World Heritage Site.

The Sui also began choosing officials by examination: the beginning of what later became the (科舉, keju). But Emperor Yang drove his people too hard. As well as digging the canal and building an eastern capital, he sent huge armies three times against Goguryeo (高句麗) in the north of the Korean peninsula; the first expedition alone is said to have mobilised more than a million men, and all three failed. Uprisings broke out everywhere, and in AD 618 Emperor Yang was killed by his own men at Yangzhou (揚州). The Sui was very like the Qin (秦): both unified China, both set up institutions with long-lasting influence, and both fell within two generations because they overworked the people.

AD 618 – 649

The founding of the Tang and Emperor Taizong

In AD 618 (李淵), a Sui aristocrat, founded the Tang dynasty at Chang'an. His second son, (李世民), had done most of the fighting to win the empire, but he was not the crown prince. In AD 626 Li Shimin ambushed and killed his elder brother (the crown prince) and his younger brother at the Xuanwu Gate (玄武門), the north gate of the palace, in what is known as the (玄武門之變). Two months later his father was forced to hand the throne to him, and he became Emperor Taizong of Tang (唐太宗).

This emperor, who came to power in such a bloody way, became one of the most praised rulers in Chinese history. He was good at choosing people and encouraged his ministers to criticise him frankly. One of them, (魏徵), remonstrated with him more than two hundred times, often leaving Taizong red-faced. After Wei Zheng died, Taizong said: "With bronze as a mirror, one can straighten one's clothes and cap; with people as a mirror, one can see one's successes and failures. I have lost a mirror." His reign brought good government and economic recovery, and is known as the (貞觀之治). In AD 630 Tang armies defeated the powerful Eastern Turks (東突厥) on the northern steppe, and the steppe peoples honoured Taizong as the (天可汗, tian kehan), the supreme leader of all their peoples.

7th – 8th centuries AD

Chang'an: a city of the world

The Tang capital, Chang'an, was one of the largest cities in the world at the time, with about a million people and an area of more than eighty square kilometres, seven or eight times the size of the old city of Xi'an (西安) today. The city was laid out neatly like a chessboard, divided by straight avenues into more than a hundred walled residential wards called fang (坊). Every day at sunset drums were beaten and the ward gates closed, and walking in the avenues at night was forbidden. The East Market (東市) and West Market (西市) were two large marketplaces, and the West Market in particular had many foreign merchants.

Chang'an was home to people from many places: (粟特) merchants from Central Asia ran the trade on the Silk Road (絲綢之路), Persians kept jewellery shops, and students from Japan and Korea came here to study. Japan sent more than ten (遣唐使), which brought back Tang institutions, writing, Buddhism and architecture; the layouts of Japan's old capitals, Nara and Kyoto, were modelled on Chang'an. Chang'an was also a city of many religions: besides Buddhism and Daoism (道教), there was Zoroastrianism and Manichaeism from Persia, Islam brought by Arab merchants, and a Christian church from Syria, which the Chinese called (景教, the "Luminous Religion"). People in the Tang loved to wear "barbarian" (hu) clothes, eat "barbarian" flatbreads and listen to music and dance from the Western Regions (西域); the court orchestra had many Central Asian instruments, one of which was the pipa (琵琶), a kind of lute.

AD 629 – 645

Xuanzang: the real "Journey to the West"

Early in the Tang, a young monk named felt that China's translations of Buddhist scriptures were full of errors, and resolved to go to India, the birthplace of Buddhism, to find the original texts. At the time the court forbade ordinary people to leave the country, so in AD 629 he slipped out of Chang'an secretly and crossed the Gobi Desert (戈壁沙漠), the Tianshan (天山) mountains and the Hindu Kush alone, reaching India by way of Central Asia (see Map 4). He studied for five years at (那爛陀寺), the greatest Buddhist centre of learning in India at the time, and became a famous scholar.

In AD 645 Xuanzang returned to Chang'an with more than 650 Buddhist texts, and was received with great ceremony by Emperor Taizong. For nearly twenty years after that he led the translation of the scriptures, and also dictated the (大唐西域記), describing the land and people of more than a hundred countries along his route. It is still an important source for the ancient history of Central Asia and India. The Big Wild Goose Pagoda (大雁塔) in Chang'an was built to house the scriptures he brought back.

More than nine hundred years later, the Ming novel (西遊記) turned Xuanzang's story into a fantasy: the Tang monk travels with the all-powerful Monkey, Sun Wukong (孫悟空), together with Pigsy (豬八戒) and Sandy (沙僧), defeating demons all the way. British readers may remember Monkey, the Japanese television series shown on the BBC in the late 1970s, which told this very story.

AD 690 – 705

Wu Zetian: the only female emperor

(武則天) began as a low-ranking consort of Emperor Taizong; after Taizong died, she became empress to his son, Emperor Gaozong (高宗). Gaozong was often ill, and Empress Wu gradually took control of the government. After Gaozong died, she put two of her own sons on the throne and removed them in turn, and in AD 690 took the throne herself, renaming the dynasty "Zhou" (周). She was the only woman in Chinese history to become emperor formally, in her own name.

Her rule was harsh: she employed cruel officials, encouraged informers, and killed large numbers of royal family members and ministers who opposed her. But she also valued the civil service examination as a way to recruit able people of humble birth, and the country was broadly stable. In AD 705, while ill, she was forced to abdicate and the Tang was restored. After her death she was buried with Gaozong, and in front of the tomb stands a stone tablet with no inscription, known as the "Wordless Stele" (無字碑). People have explained it in various ways, for example as leaving her achievements and failings for later generations to judge.

Sui – Tang

The civil service examination: choosing officials by exam

From the Han dynasty onwards, officials were mainly recommended by local authorities, and by the Northern and Southern Dynasties almost every chance of office had been monopolised by the great families. The , begun under the Sui and Tang, allowed educated men to become officials by passing an exam. The most prestigious in the Tang was the jinshi (進士) degree, which tested poetry and views on current affairs. Only twenty or thirty candidates passed each year, and there was a saying, "fifty is young for a jinshi": passing at fifty still counted as young.

Even so, under the Tang the sons of great families still made up most officials, and the examination was only one way in; it was not until the Song dynasty (宋朝) that the examination became the main route into government (see Era 6). The system lasted until it was abolished in 1905. In the nineteenth century Britain began to choose its civil servants by open competitive examination, and some scholars believe this was inspired by China's examination system.

AD 701 – 770

Tang poetry

The Tang was the golden age of Chinese poetry: the Complete Tang Poems (全唐詩), compiled under the Qing (清朝), contains nearly fifty thousand poems by more than two thousand poets. The two most famous poets are (李白) and (杜甫). Li Bai was bold and free-spirited, loved wine and travel, and wrote lines of soaring imagination; he is called the "Immortal of Poetry" (詩仙). Almost every Chinese child can recite his lines: "Before my bed, the bright moonlight; I wonder if it is frost on the ground. I lift my head and gaze at the bright moon; I lower my head and think of home." Du Fu lived through the wars that turned the Tang from glory to decline, and his poems are full of compassion for the suffering of ordinary people; he is called the "Sage of Poetry" (詩聖).

Woodblock printing also appeared under the Tang: a whole page of text was carved in reverse on a wooden block, inked and printed on paper. The oldest surviving printed book in the world that bears a clear date is a scroll of the Buddhist (金剛經), printed in AD 868. It was found at Dunhuang and is now in the British Library in London.

AD 712 – 763

The golden age and the An Lushan Rebellion

The first part of the reign of (唐玄宗), known as the (開元盛世), was the most prosperous time of the Tang. Du Fu remembered how then "the rice ran rich with oil, the millet was white, and public and private granaries were all full". But in his later years Xuanzong gave himself up to pleasure and doted on his consort (楊貴妃); it is said that to bring her fresh lychees, he had them carried from the south to Chang'an by relays of fast horses. The government fell into the hands of Yang Guozhong (楊國忠), her cousin, and others.

To defend its long frontiers, the Tang set up large military regions on the borders, each under a (節度使, jiedushi) who controlled the army, finances and administration at once. One of them was (安祿山), of Sogdian and Turkic descent, who was military governor of three regions at once and commanded more than a hundred thousand crack troops. In AD 755 he rose in revolt, claiming to be moving against Yang Guozhong, and quickly captured Luoyang and Chang'an; Xuanzong fled in haste towards Sichuan. On the way, the soldiers escorting him mutinied, killed Yang Guozhong, and forced Xuanzong to order Yang Guifei's death.

The rebellion was led first by An Lushan and then by his general Shi Siming (史思明), and lasted eight years; it is known as the (安史之亂). The Tang's registered population fell from about 53 million in AD 754 to under 17 million a few years later (see the note under Figure 2). This does not mean that two thirds of the people died: many fled or simply dropped off the household registers. But it shows how hard the state had been hit. Although the Tang eventually put down the rebellion, from then on it was in decline.

AD 763 – 907

The decline and fall of the Tang

After the An Lushan Rebellion, military governors all over the country used their armies to make themselves independent; some even passed their posts from father to son, becoming almost independent kingdoms. This is known as the (藩鎮割據, fanzhen geju). At court, eunuchs took control of the emperor's palace guard and could even put emperors on the throne or remove them. In the Western Regions, Tang power was replaced by that of Tibet (吐蕃) and the Uyghurs (回鶻); in AD 751 a Tang army was defeated by the army of the Arab Abbasid Caliphate at (怛羅斯) in Central Asia, marking the end of Tang expansion into Central Asia.

In AD 845 Emperor Wuzong of Tang (唐武宗) ordered more than four thousand monasteries to be pulled down and forced more than two hundred thousand monks and nuns to return to lay life, in what is known as the "Huichang persecution of Buddhism" (會昌滅佛). Foreign religions other than Buddhism were also attacked, and the open atmosphere of the Tang gradually faded. In the late Tang, (黃巢), a former salt smuggler, led a huge rebellion that fought its way across the whole country; according to Arab accounts, when he took Guangzhou (廣州) he killed large numbers of foreign merchants. The rebellion was put down, but by then the Tang existed in name only. In AD 907 Zhu Wen (朱溫), a military governor who had once been one of Huang Chao's generals before going over to the Tang, deposed the last Tang emperor. The Tang dynasty was over, and China was divided once again.

Daily life

How people lived

A day in Chang'an

Before dawn, the city gates and ward gates opened to the sound of drums, and officials rode to the morning audience at court. At noon, drums opened the East and West Markets, where merchants sold silk, spices, jewels and horses, and in the taverns women from Central Asia served the customers. Before sunset the drums sounded again and people had to hurry back to their own wards; anyone caught walking in the avenues at night would be beaten by the soldiers on night patrol. Festivals such as the Lantern Festival (元宵節) were the exception, when the city stayed open all night to view the lanterns.

Women in the Tang

Tang women were much freer than in later times. Aristocratic women could ride horses and play polo, go out dressed in men's clothes, and some took part in politics. Divorce and remarriage were not unusual; even princesses remarried. The women shown in Tang pottery figures and wall paintings, with their hair piled high, long brightly coloured skirts and full faces, are seen as the ideal of beauty of the time.

The life of farmers

In the early Tang the state ran the (均田制, juntianzhi): it gave farmers land to cultivate, and in return they paid the state in grain and cloth and did labour service. The system came from the Northern Wei and worked well after wars, when people were few and empty land was plentiful. But by the mid-Tang, with the population growing and land being bought up by the powerful, it could no longer be kept going, and was replaced by taxes based on property.

Key people

Who's who

  • Zhuge Liang (諸葛亮)Chief minister of Shu Han, AD 181–234

    Served Liu Bei and his son, giving everything "until his dying breath". In the Romance of the Three Kingdoms he is a sage who can summon the wind and rain, and for Chinese people he is the very picture of wisdom.

  • Emperor Xiaowen of Northern Wei (北魏孝文帝)Xianbei emperor, AD 467–499

    Moved the capital to Luoyang and carried out sweeping reforms to adopt Han Chinese ways. A key figure in the mixing of peoples.

  • Emperor Yang of Sui (隋煬帝, Yang Guang)Sui emperor, AD 569–618

    Dug the Grand Canal and attacked Goguryeo three times. He lost his dynasty by overworking the people and is remembered as a tyrant, but the institutions and canal he left behind had a lasting influence.

  • Emperor Taizong of Tang (唐太宗, Li Shimin)Tang emperor, AD 598–649

    Seized the throne in the Xuanwu Gate Incident, yet became a wise ruler famous for accepting criticism and choosing good people, and created the Reign of Zhenguan.

  • Xuanzang (玄奘)Monk and translator, AD 602–664

    Travelled alone to India and back over seventeen years, bringing home a great many Buddhist texts and leading their translation. The real person behind the Tang monk in Journey to the West.

  • Wu Zetian (武則天)China's only female emperor, AD 624–705

    Rose from consort to empress and finally declared herself emperor, founding the Zhou dynasty and reigning for fifteen years.

  • Li Bai (李白) and Du Fu (杜甫)Poets, AD 701–762; 712–770

    China's two greatest poets, one bold and romantic, the other sombre and realistic. The two knew each other and wrote poems for one another.

Myth or fact

What people often get wrong

  • "The Romance of the Three Kingdoms is the history of the Three Kingdoms."No. The Romance of the Three Kingdoms is a Ming novel, often said to be "seven parts fact, three parts fiction". Famous episodes such as Zhuge Liang's "Empty Fort Strategy" (空城計) and "borrowing arrows with straw boats" (草船借箭) cannot be found in the historical records, or originally involved other people. To learn about the real Three Kingdoms, read the Records of the Three Kingdoms (三國志, Sanguozhi), a history written by Chen Shou (陳壽) of the Western Jin (西晉).
  • "In the Tang, fat was beautiful."Too simple. In paintings of court ladies and pottery figures from the mid-Tang, women are indeed fuller-figured, but this was only the fashion of one period. Images of women from the early Tang are rather slender, and there is no evidence that Yang Guifei was fat.
  • "The Tang royal family was purely Han Chinese."No. The mother of the Tang founder Li Yuan, and the mother and wife of Emperor Taizong, all came from aristocratic families with strong Xianbei roots. The ruling class of the Sui and Tang was itself a product of the mixing of peoples under the Northern Dynasties, and this is one reason the Tang was so open to foreign cultures.
  • "Yang Guifei caused the An Lushan Rebellion."Blaming a woman. The underlying causes of the rebellion were the excessive power of the military governors, the imbalance between troops on the frontier and troops at the centre, and corruption at court. The idea that beautiful women bring ruin to states is very common in Chinese histories, but it is not fair.
How we know

A library in a cave

Official histories

Every dynasty of this era has an official history compiled by the state, such as the Records of the Three Kingdoms, the Book of Jin (晉書) and the Book of Sui (隋書); the Tang has two, the Old Book of Tang (舊唐書) and the New Book of Tang (新唐書). But the histories of the Northern and Southern Dynasties often run each other down: the south called northerners "braided barbarians" (索虜), and the north called southerners "island savages" (島夷), so they need to be read with care.

The Dunhuang Library Cave

In 1900 Wang Yuanlu (王圓籙), a Daoist priest who looked after the Mogao Caves, chanced upon a small cave that had been sealed for about nine hundred years. Inside were about fifty thousand manuscripts and printed items from the fourth to the eleventh centuries, including Buddhist scriptures, contracts, account books, letters and schoolchildren's exercises. In the early twentieth century many of them were bought cheaply and taken away by foreign explorers such as Aurel Stein from Britain and Paul Pelliot from France, and today they are scattered across London, Paris, St Petersburg, Beijing and elsewhere. This is still a sore point for many Chinese people.

Tombs and wall paintings

The tombs of Tang royalty and aristocrats contain many wall paintings and pottery figures showing court life, polo matches, processions and foreign envoys. Among the brightly coloured "Tang three-colour" (唐三彩, sancai) pottery figures, Central Asian merchants riding camels are common: direct evidence of a thriving Silk Road.

Key terms

Terms from this era

Three Kingdoms (三國)
The period after the Eastern Han when the three states of Wei, Shu and Wu existed side by side (220–280).
Five Barbarians and Sixteen Kingdoms (五胡十六國)
The period after the collapse of the Western Jin when the north was ruled by states of many different peoples.
Northern and Southern Dynasties (南北朝)
The period when Han Chinese courts in the south faced courts founded by the Xianbei and other peoples in the north (420–589).
Grand Canal (大運河)
The artificial waterway dug under the Sui, centred on Luoyang, linking north and south.
Civil service examination (科舉)
The system of choosing officials by examination, begun under the Sui and only abolished in 1905.
Military governor (節度使)
The commander of a large Tang frontier military region, holding military and civil power; the root of the An Lushan Rebellion and of the later regional warlords.
Embassies to Tang China (遣唐使)
Official missions sent by Japan to learn from the Tang.
Tang people (唐人)
Overseas Chinese often call themselves "Tang people", which is where the Chinese name for Chinatown, "Tang people's street" (唐人街), comes from.
Hong Kong

Tuen Mun in the Tang

Tang Guangzhou was China's biggest port for foreign trade, with a constant stream of merchant ships from Arabia, Persia and Southeast Asia. Every foreign ship sailing to or from Guangzhou had to pass Tuen Mun (屯門), outside the mouth of the Pearl River (珠江). Around AD 736 the Tang set up a military garrison at Tuen Mun, with about two thousand troops, to protect the shipping lane and the merchant ships (see Map 3). The Tang writers Han Yu (韓愈) and Liu Yuxi (劉禹錫) both mention Tuen Mun in their poems. This is one of the earliest records of troops stationed in the Hong Kong area.

Legend has it that in the Northern and Southern Dynasties an eminent monk, Pui To (杯渡禪師), who crossed the sea in a wooden cup, practised his religion on Castle Peak (青山) in Tuen Mun; today's Castle Peak Monastery (青山禪院) and Pui To Rock (杯渡岩) on the mountain take their names from him. But this is legend.

Meanwhile in Europe and the world

Empires collapse and new powers rise

The fall of Rome

In AD 410 the Visigoths sacked the city of Rome; in AD 476 the Western Roman Empire came to an end. This was about a hundred years after the Western Jin was overthrown by northern peoples and the Jin court moved south, and the two are often compared. The difference is that China was reunited in 589, while Western Europe split into many kingdoms, as it remains today. Roman troops left Britain around AD 410, and afterwards the Anglo-Saxons founded several kingdoms in England.

The Islamic empire and Talas

In the seventh century Islam arose in the Arabian peninsula, and the Arab empire expanded rapidly into Central Asia, where it met Tang power. In AD 751 the two armies fought at Talas, on today's border between Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan, and the Tang army was defeated. In 762 the Abbasid Caliphate founded a new capital, Baghdad, which ranked alongside Chang'an as one of the largest cities in the world.

The Vikings and Iona

In the later Tang period, the Vikings began raiding the coasts of Britain. In AD 795 they first plundered the monastery on Iona, off Scotland's west coast; the magnificent illuminated gospel book, the Book of Kells, was very probably made by monks there in this period. In AD 800 Charlemagne, King of the Franks, was crowned emperor in Rome.

Places to visit

  • Mogao Caves (莫高窟)Dunhuang, Gansu

    A World Heritage Site with more than seven hundred caves and over 45,000 square metres of wall paintings, painted from the Northern Dynasties right through to the Yuan.

  • Longmen Grottoes (龍門石窟)Luoyang, Henan

    A World Heritage Site. The great Vairocana Buddha of Fengxian Temple is seventeen metres tall; its face is said to have been carved in the likeness of Wu Zetian, but there is no reliable evidence for this.

  • Yungang Grottoes (雲岡石窟)Datong, Shanxi

    A World Heritage Site: giant early Northern Wei Buddhas with clear Central Asian and Indian influence.

  • Big Wild Goose Pagoda (大雁塔), Xi'an Stele Forest (西安碑林) and Shaanxi History Museum (陝西歷史博物館)Xi'an, Shaanxi

    The pagoda was built to house Xuanzang's scriptures; the Stele Forest holds the Nestorian Stele (大秦景教流行中國碑), which records the spread of Jingjiao Christianity in the Tang; the museum has many Tang wall paintings and three-colour pottery figures.

  • Yangzhou (揚州) and the Grand Canal (大運河)Yangzhou, Jiangsu, and elsewhere

    A World Heritage Site. Yangzhou is where Emperor Yang of Sui met his death, and was one of the richest trading cities of the Tang.

  • Castle Peak Monastery (青山禪院)Tuen Mun, Hong Kong

    Linked by legend to the monk Pui To of the Northern and Southern Dynasties, and one of Hong Kong's oldest Buddhist temples.

  • British Library and British MuseumLondon · Free

    The British Library holds the Diamond Sutra of AD 868 and a large collection of Dunhuang manuscripts; the British Museum has Tang three-colour camel figures and silk paintings from Dunhuang.

The one thing to rememberAfter the Han, China was divided for more than three hundred years, while northern peoples and Han Chinese gradually mixed and Buddhism took root; the Sui reunited the country and dug the Grand Canal, and the Tang became an open, international golden age until the An Lushan Rebellion sent it into decline.

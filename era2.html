Era 2

Shang and Western Zhou

c. 1600 – 771 BC

This is the first age in China to leave behind its own writing. The kings of the Shang dynasty (商) consulted their ancestors and the spirits before doing anything, by divination, and carved their questions on turtle shells and ox bones. These "oracle bone inscriptions" (甲骨文) are the ancestors of the Chinese characters used today. Shang craftsmen also cast the finest bronzes in the world at that time. Later the Zhou (周), a people from the west, overthrew the Shang. They put forward the idea of the "Mandate of Heaven" (天命, tianming): that Heaven gives the world only to a ruler with virtue. They also handed out land to their relatives, who set up one small state after another. Writing, ancestor worship, the Mandate of Heaven and politics built on the family all began in this age, and shaped China for three thousand years.

Before we begin

Two dynasties, and a history with writing

This era covers two . First came the (商), from about 1600 to 1046 BC, which ruled the plains of the middle and lower Yellow River (黃河), in what are now Henan (河南), Hebei (河北) and Shandong (山東). Then came the (周). The Zhou people originally lived further west, in the valley of the Wei River (渭河, in today's Shaanxi, 陝西), and defeated the Shang in 1046 BC. The Zhou dynasty lasted for nearly eight hundred years, and historians divide it into two parts. This era covers the first part, the (西周), up to 771 BC.

Unlike Era 1, we at last have writing set down by people of the time themselves: the Shang's , and the Zhou's (金文), cast into bronze vessels. Even so, dates before 841 BC are still only scholars' approximate calculations, and different studies can differ by several decades.

Bear in mind that neither the Shang nor the Zhou was a country with clear borders in today's sense. At the centre of each was a royal house and its capital, surrounded by tribes large and small that sometimes submitted and sometimes rebelled (see Map 1).

c. 1600 – 1300 BC

The founding of the Shang, and a capital that kept moving

According to later histories, (湯), leader of the Shang people, overthrew (桀), the cruel last ruler of the (夏), and founded the Shang dynasty. Later generations honoured Tang as a sage king who won the world through benevolence and virtue, but this story was only written down more than a thousand years later, and its details cannot be proved.

The great early Shang city that archaeologists have found is the (鄭州商城), in the centre of Zhengzhou, today the capital of Henan province. Its walls were nearly seven kilometres round, built of (夯土, hangtu): earth poured layer by layer into a wooden frame and pounded hard. Parts of the walls still stand beside Zhengzhou's roads today.

The early Shang kings moved their capital many times, perhaps because of floods, war or struggles within the royal family. In about 1300 BC the Shang king (盤庚) moved the capital to a place called Yin (殷), today's Anyang (安陽) in northern Henan, and for more than two hundred years after that it never moved again. That is why the Shang dynasty is also called the (殷), or "Yin-Shang".

c. 1300 – 1046 BC

Yinxu: the last Shang capital

After the Shang fell, the city of Yin became a ruin, which later people called (殷墟), "the Ruins of Yin". The site covers about thirty-six square kilometres, far larger than the whole of Edinburgh's Old Town. Here archaeologists have found palaces and ancestral temples built on rammed-earth platforms, more than ten huge royal tombs, workshops for casting bronze and carving bone, and (車馬坑) holding complete chariots with their horses.

Horses and horse-drawn chariots appear suddenly at Yinxu only around 1200 BC. They had never been seen in China before, and most likely came from the northern steppes, passed along through many hands. In the same period, the armies of Egypt and the Middle East were also fighting with chariots.

Yinxu also reveals the cruel side of the Shang. Around the royal tombs are thousands of sacrificial pits, holding people killed as offerings to the ancestors, many of them foreigners captured in war. This kind of (人祭, renji) was very common under the Shang, and only declined greatly under the Zhou.

c. 1250 – 1046 BC

Asking the ancestors: oracle bone divination

The Shang believed that their dead ancestors still watched over their descendants from another world, and could send either blessings or disaster. Above the ancestors was a supreme god who controlled wind, rain and war, called (帝) or "Shangdi" (上帝). So whatever the Shang king did, he first used to ask the ancestors and spirits what they thought. Will it rain tomorrow? Will this campaign be won? Will the queen's childbirth go well? The king has toothache: is one of the ancestors causing it?

This is how the divination worked (see Figure 2). First a turtle's under-shell or an ox's shoulder blade was ground smooth, and rows of small hollows were chiselled into the back. During the divination a red-hot stick was pressed into a hollow, and with a "crack" a split burst out on the front of the bone. The diviner, called a (貞人), and the king read good or bad fortune from the shape of the crack, and then carved the date, the question, and sometimes the later outcome, next to the crack. The Chinese character for divination today, 卜 (bu), looks exactly like a crack; its sound may also imitate the noise of the bone cracking.

These carvings are the oracle bone inscriptions. About 150,000 pieces of inscribed bone and shell have been dug up at Yinxu so far, bearing more than four thousand different characters, of which about a thousand or more have been deciphered by scholars. Oracle bone script was already a mature writing system able to record the language in full, and it is the direct ancestor of today's Chinese characters (see Figure 3). Chinese is the only ancient script in the world that has remained in use from more than three thousand years ago to the present day; Egyptian hieroglyphs and Mesopotamian cuneiform could no longer be read by anyone more than fifteen hundred years ago.

c. 1600 – 1046 BC

Bronze: the metal of power

is a metal made by melting copper and tin together (sometimes with lead), and is harder than pure copper. Shang bronzes were not mainly farm tools. They were ritual vessels for offerings to the ancestors, holding wine, meat and grain, along with weapons and fittings for chariots. Whoever owned more, and larger, bronzes had the higher status.

Shang craftsmen used a distinctive (塊範法, kuaifan). They first made a clay model, pressed clay moulds around the outside in several sections, and then poured molten bronze into the gap between the moulds and an inner core. This method could cast enormous vessels with extremely fine decoration. The most famous is the (后母戊鼎), more than 130 centimetres tall and weighing about 830 kilograms, as much as ten or more adults: the heaviest ancient Chinese bronze that survives.

The (鼎) was originally a large three- or four-legged cauldron for cooking meat, and later became a symbol of royal power and of the state. In Chinese today, to say someone is "asking about the ding" (問鼎) of a championship means they are trying to seize the top place.

c. 1250 – 1192 BC

Wu Ding and Fu Hao: a royal couple

Shang kings' names often contain characters such as jia, yi, bing or ding. These are the (天干, tiangan), used for counting days, and they mark the day on which each king received his offerings. (武丁) was the most powerful Shang king. He is said to have reigned for fifty-nine years and campaigned in every direction, and he left more oracle bone records than any other Shang king.

Wu Ding had several queens, the most famous of whom was (婦好). The oracle bones record that she not only led sacrifices but also led armies into battle herself, on one occasion commanding as many as thirteen thousand men. Wu Ding often divined about her too: would her illness get better? Would her childbirth go well?

In 1976 archaeologists found Fu Hao's tomb at Yinxu. It is the only Shang royal tomb that had never been robbed and whose owner can be identified. It held nearly two thousand grave goods, including more than four hundred bronzes, more than seven hundred jades, and nearly seven thousand , which were then treasured as wealth. Some of the bronze weapons are inscribed with the name "Fu Hao", confirming that she was a woman general.

c. 1200 – 1000 BC

Sanxingdui: another world beyond the Shang

The Shang was not the whole of China at that time. In the Sichuan Basin (四川盆地) in the southwest, more than a thousand kilometres from Yinxu, was a civilisation that later people called (古蜀), which developed a bronze culture completely different from the Shang's.

In 1986, at (三星堆) in Guanghan (廣漢), Sichuan (四川), two large pits packed with objects were discovered. Out of them came giant bronze masks with eyes bulging forward, a bronze sacred tree nearly four metres tall, a life-size standing bronze figure and gold face masks. Nothing shaped like these had ever been seen anywhere else in China. From 2019 archaeologists found six more pits next to them. The people of Sanxingdui knew how to obtain bronze materials from far away, but they left no writing at all. What they worshipped, and why they broke these objects, burned them and then buried them, is still a mystery.

c. 1100 – 1046 BC

The rise of the Zhou and the Battle of Muye

West of the Shang, on the (周原) in the Wei River valley, lived the Zhou people. They had begun as a tribe on the Shang's western edge, and had once paid tribute to the Shang king. Their leader Ji Chang (姬昌), known to later generations as (周文王), gradually united the western tribes during his rule, pushed his power eastwards, and moved his capital to (鎬京), near today's Xi'an (西安).

After King Wen died, his son Ji Fa (姬發), (周武王), led the Zhou army and its allies east. With the help of his military adviser (姜太公), he met the Shang army in a decisive battle at Muye (牧野), near the Shang capital: the (牧野之戰). According to the Zhou accounts, the Shang army, though larger, turned against its own side on the battlefield. The last Shang king, (帝辛), fled back to his capital, climbed a high tower and burned himself to death. A bronze vessel dug up in 1976, the (利簋), has an inscription recording that King Wu took the Shang capital on the morning of a "jiazi" day. It is the only evidence of this war from the time itself.

Di Xin is more often known to later generations as "King Zhou" (紂王). "Zhou" (紂, a different word from the dynasty's name) was an insulting name given to him by the Zhou and later writers, who also portrayed him as a tyrant lost in drink and women who tortured loyal ministers. Most of these stories come from the winners and cannot be fully trusted (see "Myth or fact").

From 1046 BC

The Mandate of Heaven: why did the Zhou have the right to rule?

A small western tribe had overthrown the mighty Shang, and the Zhou needed to explain to the world why they should be the ones to rule. Their answer was the . The Zhou called their highest god "Heaven" (天, tian). Heaven, they said, gives the power to rule the world to a virtuous ruler; if a ruler becomes cruel and lawless, Heaven takes back the mandate and gives it to someone else who is virtuous. The early Shang rulers had been virtuous, so they received the mandate; Di Xin lost his virtue, so the mandate passed to the Zhou.

This is a little like the "divine right of kings" in medieval Europe: both say a ruler's power comes from above. But there is one important difference: the Mandate of Heaven could be lost. That made it a double-edged sword. New dynasties used it to prove they were legitimate, and rebels used it to prove they were right to overthrow a tyrant. For the next three thousand years, every change of dynasty in China was explained by the Mandate of Heaven; frequent natural disasters and widespread suffering were often seen as signs that a dynasty had lost it.

Another important early Zhou bronze is the (何尊). Its inscription contains the four characters 宅茲中國, meaning "to settle in this centre of the world". This is the earliest known use of the word (中國), today the Chinese name for China. At that time it meant the Central Plain (中原) around Luoyang (洛陽), not the name of a country.

c. 1043 – 1000 BC

The Duke of Zhou and fengjian: dividing the world among relatives

King Wu died not long after conquering the Shang. His son, (成王), was still young, so King Wu's younger brother, the (周公), ruled in his place. Several of the Duke's brothers refused to accept this, and even joined forces with Di Xin's son to rebel, in what is known as the (三監之亂). It took the Duke of Zhou three years to put down the revolt. He then built a second capital in the east, (洛邑, today's Luoyang), to keep watch over the former Shang lands. When King Cheng grew up, the Duke of Zhou handed power back to him. Later Confucians (儒家) regarded the Duke of Zhou as loyal and selfless, the ideal statesman.

Zhou lands were too vast for the king to govern directly, so the Zhou used (分封): granting land, and the people on it, to royal relatives and to those who had served well, who then went out and set up small states of their own, known as (諸侯, zhuhou). According to ancient texts, more than seventy states were granted in the early Zhou, more than fifty of them ruled by relatives of the king who shared his surname. For example, the Duke of Zhou's son was given (魯, today's Qufu (曲阜) in Shandong, later the home town of Confucius, 孔子), Jiang Taigong was given (齊), King Cheng's younger brother was given (晉), and another senior minister, the Duke of Shao (召公), was given (燕, near today's Beijing, 北京). Within his own state each lord could run his own affairs, but he had to attend the Zhou king's court regularly, send tribute, and bring troops to help in time of war (see Figure 4).

Fengjian is often translated into English as "feudalism", and it really does resemble the system in medieval Europe in which kings granted land to nobles in return for loyalty and military service. The biggest difference is that Zhou fengjian was based on blood: most of the lords were the Zhou king's own brothers, uncles, sons and nephews.

1046 – 771 BC

Clan rules and ritual: the family is the state

The Zhou organised noble families through the (宗法), or clan system. At its heart was the rule that the (嫡長子, dizhangzi) inherited his father's position and main property; other sons founded branch lines, of lower rank, but still had to respect the main line. The Zhou king was the greatest "main line" in the world, and the lords were branches; within each vassal state, the lord in turn was the main line. In this way the state was like one vast family, with "family" and "state" bound together: the Chinese word for country, guojia (國家), literally "state-family", reflects this idea. Inheritance by the eldest son is similar to the way the British throne and noble titles have traditionally passed to the eldest son.

The Zhou also laid down a whole system of (禮), or ritual, setting out how many bronze vessels people of different rank could use in sacrifices, banquets, weddings and funerals, where they should stand and what music should be played. The purpose of ritual was to make everyone clear about their place and to keep social order. About five hundred years later, the order represented by these Zhou rituals was exactly what Confucius most longed for.

841 – 770 BC

Decline: the first year of Gonghe and the fall of the Western Zhou

After several generations the Zhou kings' power gradually weakened, and the blood ties between the lords and the king grew more and more distant. (周厲王) was greedy and cruel and banned the people from criticising him; it is said that when people met in the street they dared only exchange glances. In 841 BC the people of the capital finally rose up and drove him out. For the next fourteen years ministers governed in his place, a period called the (共和) regency. This year, "the first year of Gonghe", is the first year in Chinese history that can be dated exactly, because the chronological tables in the (史記, Shiji), by the Han dynasty (漢) historian Sima Qian (司馬遷), record events year by year from this point. More than two thousand years later the word gonghe was borrowed to translate the English word "republic", and that is where the "Republic" in today's "People's Republic of China" (中華人民共和國) comes from.

About seventy years later, (周幽王) became infatuated with his consort (褒姒), and deposed his queen and the crown prince. The queen's father, the Marquis of Shen (申侯), was furious, and joined forces with the (犬戎), a people from the northwest, to storm Haojing, killing King You in 771 BC. The next year the deposed crown prince took the throne as King Ping of Zhou (周平王) and moved the capital east to Luoyi.

Historians use this move as the dividing line. Before it, the capital was at Haojing in the west, and the period is called the Western Zhou; after it, the capital was at Luoyi in the east, and the period is called the (東周). After the move east, the Zhou king kept only his title, while the lords fought each other for supremacy, and China entered the next era: the Spring and Autumn (春秋) and Warring States (戰國) periods.

Daily life

How people lived

Nobles in the capital at Yin, c. 1200 BC

Nobles lived in timber-framed halls built on rammed-earth platforms. They used bronze vessels to offer wine and meat to their ancestors, and after the sacrifice everyone feasted together. Shang nobles were very fond of drink: their tombs often contain all kinds of wine vessels, such as the jue (爵) and gu (觚), and the Zhou later claimed that the Shang had fallen because of drunkenness. Nobles hunted from chariots and wore jade ornaments. When they died they were buried, according to rank, with bronzes, jades and even people killed to accompany them.

Farmers and craftsmen

The great majority of people were farmers, living in simple houses half sunk into the ground and roofed with thatch. With wooden tools and stone sickles they grew foxtail and broomcorn millet, and later wheat too, often working together on nobles' land. Around Yinxu were specialist bronze workshops, bone workshops and pottery kilns, where families of craftsmen handed down the same trade from generation to generation.

Educating Zhou noble sons

Boys of Zhou noble families had to learn the "Six Arts" (六藝): rites, music, archery, chariot driving, writing and arithmetic. They had to memorise how to bow, what to wear and what to say on each different occasion. This is a little like the riding, fighting skills and court manners that European knights later had to learn.

Key people

Who's who

  • Tang (湯)Founder of the Shang, c. 1600 BC

    The legendary leader of the Shang people who overthrew Jie, the tyrant of the Xia. Later generations saw him as a sage king who won the world through benevolence and virtue.

  • Wu Ding (武丁)Shang king, reigned c. 1250–1192 BC

    King at the height of Shang power. He is said to have reigned for fifty-nine years and campaigned in every direction, and left more oracle bone records than any other king.

  • Fu Hao (婦好)Queen of Wu Ding, c. 1200 BC

    A woman general who led sacrifices and commanded armies, the first woman military leader in China's written records. Her tomb is the only unrobbed Shang royal tomb at Yinxu whose owner can be identified.

  • Di Xin (帝辛), "King Zhou" (紂王)Last Shang king, died 1046 BC

    Burned himself to death after defeat at Muye. Later writers made him China's model tyrant, but these descriptions come mostly from the Zhou and from even later authors.

  • King Wen of Zhou (周文王, Ji Chang)Zhou leader

    United the western tribes and laid the groundwork for the Zhou conquest of the Shang. "King Wen" is an honorary title added after his death, meaning "the king of civil virtue".

  • King Wu of Zhou (周武王, Ji Fa)Founder of the Zhou, conquered the Shang in 1046 BC

    Son of King Wen. He defeated the Shang army at the Battle of Muye and founded the Zhou dynasty, but died not long after the conquest.

  • Duke of Zhou (周公, Ji Dan)Younger brother of King Wu

    Ruled on behalf of the young King Cheng, put down the Rebellion of the Three Guardians, built Luoyi, and established the fengjian system and the rites and music, before handing power back to King Cheng. Confucius said he often dreamed of the Duke of Zhou.

Myth or fact

What people often get wrong

  • "King Zhou built a lake of wine and a forest of meat, doted on Daji and lived in debauchery, so he lost his kingdom."Largely a later story. These descriptions appear mostly in texts written after the Zhou, and the later the book, the more exaggerated the account. Episodes such as Daji (妲己) being a fox spirit who bewitched King Zhou, and gods joining the war between Shang and Zhou, come from The Investiture of the Gods (封神演義), a Ming dynasty (明) fantasy novel that has recently been made into films. Even Confucius's student Zigong (子貢) said that King Zhou's wickedness was not as bad as the stories claimed. The oracle bones show that the last Shang kings were still diligent about sacrifices and campaigns.
  • "King You lit the beacon fires to make Bao Si laugh, tricking his lords, and in the end lost his kingdom."Very likely a later invention. The story appears in the Records of the Grand Historian. But among a batch of Warring States bamboo slips acquired by Tsinghua University (清華大學) in 2008 is a history called the Xinian (繫年), which describes the fall of the Western Zhou without any mention of beacon fires: it says only that King You attacked the state of Shen, and Shen struck back in alliance with the Quanrong. Some scholars also point out that a system of beacon towers for sending alarms may not have appeared until a later age.
  • "The Chinese word for merchant, shangren (商人, literally 'Shang people'), arose because after the Shang fell, its surviving people had to turn to trade."Popular but unproven. It is an entertaining idea and often repeated, but there is no reliable evidence for it, and scholars still explain the origin of the word in different ways.
  • "The Shang was a slave society."Disputed. The Shang certainly carried out large-scale human sacrifice, and many people were forced to work. But the label "slave society" comes from a twentieth-century theory of history that divided all societies into fixed stages. Many scholars think most Shang labourers were not slaves.
How we know

Writing from the medicine shop

The discovery of the oracle bones

More than a hundred years ago, farmers at Anyang in Henan often dug up pieces of bone covered in marks in their fields, and sold them to medicine shops as "dragon bones", an ingredient in Chinese medicine. According to the popular account, in 1899 Wang Yirong (王懿榮), a Beijing official and expert in ancient writing, noticed ancient characters on bones among his medicines and began buying and studying them. In 1917 the scholar Wang Guowei (王國維) used the oracle bones to work out the names and order of the Shang kings, and found that they broadly matched the list of Shang kings recorded by Sima Qian in the Records of the Grand Historian a thousand years later. This proved that the Shiji's account of the Shang was not invented.

Excavating Yinxu

From 1928 to 1937 the Academia Sinica (中央研究院) carried out fifteen seasons of excavation at Yinxu, finding palaces, royal tombs and large numbers of oracle bones. This was the first large-scale scientific excavation in China led by Chinese scholars. It stopped when war with Japan broke out. In 1949 the Academia Sinica moved to Taiwan (台灣), and many of the finds from Yinxu are still kept in Taipei (台北) today.

Bronze inscriptions and ancient books

Zhou nobles often cast long texts into bronze vessels, recording appointments by the king, rewards, wars and even lawsuits. The Mao Gong ding (毛公鼎) in the National Palace Museum (國立故宮博物院) in Taipei has an inscription of nearly five hundred characters, and is a Western Zhou document in its own right. In addition, the Book of Documents (尚書, Shangshu) preserves some early Zhou proclamations, and the Book of Songs (詩經, Shijing) collects poems from the Western Zhou to the Spring and Autumn period. They were edited by later hands, but still keep much of the language and thinking of the time.

Key terms

Terms from this era

Oracle bone inscriptions (甲骨文)
Shang writing carved on turtle shells and ox bones, mostly records of divination; the ancestor of today's Chinese characters.
Bronze inscriptions (金文)
Writing cast or carved on bronze vessels. It already existed under the Shang, and was most common under the Zhou.
Ding (鼎)
A large bronze cauldron with three or four legs, which later became a symbol of royal power and of the state.
Mandate of Heaven (天命)
The idea that Heaven gives the right to rule to a virtuous ruler; if the ruler loses his virtue, the mandate passes to someone else.
Fengjian (分封)
The Zhou king's granting of land and people to relatives and loyal servants, who set up vassal states.
Zongfa (宗法)
The clan system centred on inheritance by the eldest son of the principal wife, binding the family and the state together.
Li (禮)
A whole set of rules for how people of different rank should behave on every kind of occasion.
Gonghe (共和)
The period of rule by ministers after King Li of Zhou was driven out in 841 BC; the word is used today to translate "republic".
Meanwhile in Europe and the world

A Bronze Age world

Tutankhamun and Fu Hao

Around the time the Shang king Pangeng moved his capital to Yin, Egypt was ruled by the boy pharaoh Tutankhamun (c. 1332–1323 BC). His tomb, found in 1922, is one of the very few pharaohs' tombs never robbed, and like the tomb of Fu Hao, found at Yinxu more than fifty years later, it preserved a great many treasures.

The Bronze Age collapse

Around 1200 BC, in the time of Wu Ding and Fu Hao, several civilisations of the eastern Mediterranean, including Mycenaean Greece and the Hittite Empire in today's Turkey, collapsed one after another within a few decades; the legendary Trojan War also belongs roughly to this period. The distant Shang was untouched, and was at the height of its power.

The birth of the alphabet

Around 1050 BC, at about the same time as the Battle of Muye, the Phoenicians on the eastern shore of the Mediterranean were using an alphabet of just twenty-two signs. It later developed into the Greek and Latin alphabets, the ancestors of the letters used to write English today. In the same period, Chinese writing kept developing along the path of one character for each meaning, a direction completely different from the alphabet.

See it for yourself

Sites and museums

  • Yinxu and the Yinxu Museum (殷墟)Anyang, Henan

    A World Heritage Site. You can visit the remains of the palaces and temples, Fu Hao's tomb and the chariot pits; the museum displays large numbers of oracle bones and bronzes.

  • Sanxingdui Museum (三星堆博物館)Guanghan, Sichuan

    Home of the bulging-eyed bronze masks, the bronze sacred tree and the gold face masks. A new building opened in 2023.

  • National Museum of China (中國國家博物館)Beijing

    The Houmuwu ding and the Li gui, which records the Battle of Muye, are both on display here.

  • Baoji Bronze Ware Museum (寶雞青銅器博物院)Baoji, Shaanxi

    Near the Zhou Plain, the Zhou people's homeland. It holds the He zun with its inscription "settle in this centre of the world" and a great many Zhou bronzes.

  • National Palace Museum (國立故宮博物院) and the Academia Sinica History Museum (中央研究院歷史文物陳列館)Taipei

    The Palace Museum holds the Mao Gong ding, with its inscription of nearly five hundred characters; the Academia Sinica museum displays oracle bones and bronzes from the 1928 to 1937 excavations at Yinxu.

  • The Burrell CollectionGlasgow, UK · Free

    The collection given by the Scottish shipping merchant Sir William Burrell, with fine Shang and Zhou bronzes and Chinese ceramics.

  • British Museum, China galleryLondon, UK · Free

    Room 33 has Shang and Zhou ritual bronzes and oracle bones.

The one thing to rememberThe Shang gave China writing and bronze; the Zhou gave China the "Mandate of Heaven" and the family-based fengjian system. Together they laid the foundations of Chinese politics and culture for the next three thousand years.

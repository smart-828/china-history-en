Era 9

Republic of China

1912 – 1949

After the fall of the Qing (清), China became one of Asia's first republics, but the new republic soon descended into fighting between warlords. In these thirty-seven years China had hardly a single year of complete peace: first the warlords carved up the country, then came the struggle between two political parties, the Nationalists and the Communists, then more than ten years of Japanese invasion, and finally civil war between the Nationalists and the Communists. At the same time, this was an age in which new ideas, a new literature and new ways of living emerged. In 1949 the Communists won the civil war and founded the People's Republic of China (中華人民共和國); the government of the Republic of China, led by the Kuomintang, withdrew to Taiwan, where it continues to this day.

Before we begin

Two parties, three wars

The main actors in this era are two political parties. The (國民黨) was founded by Sun Yat-sen and is often called the Nationalists in English; the (共產黨) was founded in 1921 and is known in English as the Communists. The two parties worked together twice, and twice went to war with each other. To make sense of this era, keep three wars in mind: the fighting between the warlords (1916–1928), the Japanese invasion (1931–1945) and the civil war between the Nationalists and the Communists (1927–1936 and 1945–1949) (see Figure 1).

Note that the did not disappear in 1949. Its government moved to Taiwan, and today Taiwan's official name is still the Republic of China. Taiwan's newspapers and government documents still use the "Republic calendar" (民國紀年): 2026, for example, is the year 115 of the Republic. So the history of this era still shapes relations between the two sides of the Taiwan Strait (台灣海峽) today.

A European reader can line it up like this: these thirty-seven years took in the First World War, the Russian Revolution, the Great Depression and the Second World War. For China, the Second World War did not begin in 1939 but earlier: Japan launched its full-scale invasion of China in 1937, and some people count from as early as 1931.

1912 – 1916

The birth of the Republic, and Yuan Shikai

On 1 January 1912 the revolutionaries founded the Republic of China in Nanjing (南京) and chose (孫中山) as provisional president. But the revolutionaries did not have enough troops; the man who really held military power was the Qing minister (袁世凱). The two sides struck a deal: Yuan would persuade the six-year-old emperor (溥儀) to abdicate, and Sun would hand the presidency over to Yuan. On 12 February 1912 the Qing emperor abdicated, and more than two thousand years of imperial rule came to an end.

In 1913 China held its first parliamentary election, and the Kuomintang won the most seats. The Kuomintang's leader, Song Jiaoren (宋教仁), was about to become prime minister when he was assassinated at Shanghai (上海) railway station; many suspected that Yuan Shikai was behind it. Yuan then dissolved the Kuomintang, did away with parliament and took all power into his own hands. In 1915 he announced the restoration of the monarchy, with himself as emperor. Province after province opposed him, and even his own subordinates would not back him. After eighty-three days he was forced to abandon the monarchy, and soon afterwards he died of illness.

1916 – 1928

The warlord era

After Yuan Shikai's death, no one was able to control the whole of China. Generals who commanded armies in different regions were known as : each carved out his own territory, collected his own taxes and fought the others. The central government in Beijing (北京), known as the , was controlled in turn by whichever warlords were strongest, and went through dozens of cabinets in little more than ten years. In 1917 one warlord even put Puyi back on the throne, but the attempt collapsed after twelve days. For ordinary people, all this meant crushing taxes and levies, looting by soldiers and year after year of war.

In the northeast, the warlord (張作霖) controlled three provinces. In the south, Sun Yat-sen set up a rival government in Guangzhou (廣州) to challenge Beijing, but he had no reliable army of his own and was driven out by local warlords several times. China was in effect broken into many pieces, much like the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms (五代十國) more than a thousand years earlier.

1915 – 1925

The New Culture Movement and the May Fourth Movement

Many young intellectuals believed that overthrowing the emperor had not changed China, because Chinese minds were still bound by old traditions. In 1915 (陳獨秀) founded the magazine (新青年), which fiercely attacked Confucian moral codes and championed "Mr De" (德先生) and "Mr Sai" (賽先生), that is, democracy and science. The scholar (胡適) argued for writing in , based on everyday speech, in place of the classical Chinese that for two thousand years only the educated could understand. Written Chinese today is the result of this movement. In 1918 the writer (魯迅) published "A Madman's Diary" (狂人日記), China's first modern short story in the vernacular. Through the eyes of a madman, it says that every page of China's history books has the words "eat people" written on it. Together, these movements are known as the .

During the First World War, China joined the Allied side in 1917 and sent about 140,000 Chinese labourers to Europe, where they dug trenches and carried ammunition on the French front. The Chinese believed that after victory they would recover the rights Germany held in Shandong (山東). But at the Paris Peace Conference in 1919, Britain, France, the United States and others agreed to hand those rights to Japan. When the news arrived, on 4 May 1919 more than three thousand students in Beijing took to the streets to protest against their government's weakness. The movement quickly spread across the country, with workers going on strike and merchants shutting their shops, and in the end the Chinese delegates refused to sign the peace treaty. This was the . Many later leaders of both the Nationalists and the Communists first entered politics through this movement.

1921 – 1927

The two parties: alliance and split

After the Russian Revolution succeeded in 1917, Russia's new regime (later the Soviet Union) began exporting revolution to China. In July 1921 a dozen or so delegates met in secret in a house in the French Concession (法租界) in Shanghai and founded the Chinese Communist Party. One of them was a young teacher from Hunan (湖南), (毛澤東). At the same time, the Soviet Union also helped Sun Yat-sen reorganise the Kuomintang, supplying weapons, money and advisers, on condition that Communists be allowed to join the Kuomintang as individuals. This was known as the . In 1924 the Kuomintang founded the (黃埔軍校) near Guangzhou to train an army of its own; its commandant was (蔣介石).

Sun Yat-sen died of illness in 1925. His (三民主義, sanmin zhuyi), nationalism, democracy and the people's livelihood, became the Kuomintang's guiding ideology. In 1926 Chiang Kai-shek led the National Revolutionary Army (國民革命軍) north from Guangzhou on the Northern Expedition (北伐, beifa), to defeat the warlords and reunify China, and it swept all before it. But tensions inside the Kuomintang, and between the Nationalists and the Communists, grew ever sharper. On 12 April 1927 Chiang suddenly ordered a purge of Communists in Shanghai, and thousands of Communists and workers were arrested and killed. This is known as the (四一二事件), and it ended the First United Front. In 1928 the Northern Expedition's armies entered Beijing, and Zhang Xueliang (張學良) in the northeast declared his loyalty to the Nanjing government: China was reunified, at least in name.

1928 – 1937

The Nanjing Decade and the Long March

Over the next ten years, the (國民政府) that the Kuomintang set up in Nanjing built railways and roads, unified the currency and developed industry and education, and Shanghai became the most glittering city in the Far East. But the government's power lay mainly in the big cities of the east. The vast countryside remained poor, and many areas were still controlled by warlords. The Kuomintang ruled as a single party and restricted freedom of speech.

Driven out of the cities, the Communists moved to the countryside and set up bases of their own in mountainous areas such as southern Jiangxi (江西), known as (蘇區, suqu). There they took land from landlords and gave it to the peasants, and formed the Red Army (紅軍). Chiang Kai-shek saw the Communists as a more urgent threat than Japan, and five times sent huge armies to encircle and wipe them out. In October 1934 the Red Army was forced to abandon its base in Jiangxi and began a great retreat, first west and then north, known as the (長征, changzheng) (see Map 2). Over a year they crossed snow-covered mountains and marshy grasslands, covering a distance said to be more than 12,000 km. A little over 80,000 set out; fewer than 10,000 were left when they reached northern Shaanxi (陝西). During the march, at a meeting in Zunyi (遵義), Guizhou (貴州), in 1935, Mao Zedong won the leadership of the Red Army. In the end the Red Army made (延安) in northern Shaanxi the centre of a new base.

1931 – 1937

The Japanese invasion and the Xi'an Incident

Ever since the First Sino-Japanese War (甲午戰爭; see Era 8), Japan had been seeking to widen its power in China. On 18 September 1931 Japanese troops stationed in the northeast blew up a section of railway near Shenyang (瀋陽), falsely blamed Chinese troops, and used this as an excuse to occupy the whole of the northeast. This is known as the (九一八事變). The next year Japan set up a puppet state in the northeast, (滿洲國), with the last emperor, Puyi, who had long since abdicated, as head of state, and later even made him its "emperor". This part of his life is told in detail in the film The Last Emperor.

As Japan pressed further and further, Chiang Kai-shek still insisted on "pacifying the interior before resisting the outside", in other words, wiping out the Communists first and resisting Japan afterwards, and more and more people grew angry. In December 1936 , the Northeastern Army (東北軍) general responsible for encircling the Red Army in the northwest, detained Chiang when he came to Xi'an to oversee the campaign, and demanded that he end the civil war and join with the Communists against Japan. This is known as the (西安事變). After negotiations Chiang agreed, and the two parties joined forces once more. Zhang Xueliang personally escorted Chiang back to Nanjing, where he was promptly detained himself; he spent more than fifty years under house arrest.

1937 – 1945

All-out war against Japan

On 7 July 1937, near the Lugou Bridge (盧溝橋, known in the West as the Marco Polo Bridge) on the southwestern outskirts of Beijing, Chinese and Japanese troops clashed, in what is known as the (七七事變). Japan at once launched a full-scale invasion, and China's (抗日戰爭) broke out in full (see Map 3). From August to November that year, Chinese troops fought the Japanese for three months in Shanghai, suffering very heavy losses but showing the world China's determination to resist. In December the Japanese army captured Nanjing, then the capital, and over the following six weeks massacred large numbers of soldiers who had laid down their arms, as well as civilians, and raped women on a massive scale. This is known as the (南京大屠殺). The number of victims is still disputed: the official Chinese figure is more than 300,000, while the postwar Tokyo Trial found that more than 200,000 were killed. A German businessman in Nanjing, , together with other Westerners, set up a "Safety Zone" (安全區) that protected more than 200,000 refugees.

The Nationalist Government moved the capital to (重慶) in Sichuan, and factories, universities and tens of millions of refugees moved west into the interior. The Japanese army occupied the big cities and transport lines of the east but could not control the vast countryside, while the Communist forces fought a guerrilla war behind Japanese lines and rapidly expanded their power. Japan bombed Chongqing for years. After Japan's surprise attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941, China formally became an ally of Britain and the United States, and the Americans sent supplies to China through Burma and over the Himalayas along the air route known as "the Hump".

In August 1945 the United States dropped two atomic bombs on Japan and the Soviet Union sent its army into the northeast, and on 15 August Japan announced its surrender. China was one of the victors. It recovered the northeast and (台灣), which Japan had ruled for fifty years, and became one of the five permanent members of the Security Council of the newly founded United Nations. But the price was terrible: by common estimates, 14 to 20 million Chinese soldiers and civilians died in the war, and about 100 million more became refugees.

1945 – 1949

The civil war

After the victory over Japan, the United States sent General Marshall to China to mediate, hoping the two parties would form a coalition government, but the talks failed, and in 1946 full-scale civil war broke out. At first the Kuomintang had more troops, as well as American aid, but the government was corrupt and inflation ran out of control: the new currency issued in 1948, the (金圓券), lost tens of thousands of times its value within a year. People had to carry banknotes in sacks to buy rice, and the urban middle class lost all faith in the government. The Communists, meanwhile, carried out (土地改革) in the countryside, giving landlords' land to the peasants. This won them the support of vast numbers of peasants, and they also won over many intellectuals and students.

From 1948 to early 1949 the two sides fought three huge, decisive campaigns in the northeast, east China and north China, known as the (三大戰役), and the Kuomintang's main forces were destroyed. In April 1949 the People's Liberation Army (解放軍) crossed the Yangtze (長江) and took Nanjing. On 1 October 1949, standing on the Gate of Heavenly Peace (Tiananmen, 天安門) in Beijing, Mao Zedong proclaimed the founding of the People's Republic of China.

In December that year Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist Government moved to Taiwan, taking with it more than a million soldiers and civilians. They also took a great many precious treasures from the Palace Museum (故宮) in Beijing and the Central Museum (中央博物院) in Nanjing, which are now kept in the National Palace Museum (故宮博物院) in Taipei (台北). Since then the two sides of the Taiwan Strait have been governed separately, right up to today.

Daily life

How people lived

Modern Shanghai

In the 1930s Shanghai was the biggest city in the Far East, with more than three million people. It had foreign-run concessions, bank buildings along the Bund (外灘), department stores, cinemas and dance halls. Women wore the restyled qipao (旗袍), film stars and pop songs were famous across the country, and Shanghai was called the "Paris of the East". But the same city was also home to large numbers of child workers who laboured more than ten hours a day in factories, and refugees living in shacks.

Hard times in the countryside

At the time about nine in ten Chinese people lived in the countryside. Many farmers were tenants who rented their land and had to hand over around half of their harvest to the landlord, while also coping with warlords' heavy taxes, bandits and natural disasters. The great drought in the northwest from 1928 to 1930 and the Henan (河南) famine of 1942 each killed millions of people through starvation.

The New Woman

In the Republican period, girls began to go to school, universities began to admit women, foot-binding was banned, and the idea that people should choose their own marriage partners gradually spread. Many educated young women left home to become teachers, journalists, doctors and writers. In the countryside, though, arranged marriages and foot-binding remained common.

Key people

Who's who

  • Sun Yat-sen (孫中山)Revolutionary, 1866–1925

    Founder of the Republic of China, who set out the Three Principles of the People. Both the Kuomintang and the Communist Party honour him: Taiwan calls him the "Father of the Nation", and mainland China calls him the "Forerunner of the Revolution".

  • Yuan Shikai (袁世凱)President of the Republic of China, 1859–1916

    Brought about the abdication of the Qing emperor. After becoming president he tried to make himself emperor, failed and died of illness, leaving the country divided among warlords.

  • Lu Xun (魯迅)Writer, 1881–1936

    Born Zhou Shuren (周樹人), he first studied medicine in Japan, then decided to use literature to change the way Chinese people thought. His best-known works include "A Madman's Diary" and "The True Story of Ah Q" (阿Q正傳). He is considered the most important writer of modern Chinese literature.

  • Chiang Kai-shek (蔣介石)Kuomintang leader, 1887–1975

    Reunified China through the Northern Expedition and led the Nationalist Government against Japan. After losing the civil war he withdrew to Taiwan, where he ruled until his death in 1975.

  • Mao Zedong (毛澤東)Communist Party leader, 1893–1976

    The son of a Hunan farming family. He won the leadership of the Communist Party during the Long March, argued for surrounding the cities from the countryside, and in 1949 founded the People's Republic of China (see Era 10).

  • Soong Ching-ling (宋慶齡)Wife of Sun Yat-sen, 1893–1981

    Born into a wealthy Shanghai merchant family, she went to university in the United States. Her younger sister, Soong Mei-ling (宋美齡), was Chiang Kai-shek's wife, and her elder sister, Soong Ai-ling (宋靄齡), married the finance minister H. H. Kung (孔祥熙). The three sisters took different political sides; Soong Ching-ling later stayed in the People's Republic of China, where she served as vice-chair of the state.

Myth or fact

What people often get wrong

  • "The 1911 Revolution created a democratic republic in one stroke."It was only a beginning. The revolution overthrew the emperor, but parliament was soon dissolved, Yuan Shikai tried to make himself emperor, and more than ten years of warlord fighting followed. Democracy never really took root.
  • "The War of Resistance against Japan lasted eight years."There are two ways of counting. Traditionally it is counted from the July 7th Incident of 1937, making eight years. Since 2017, mainland Chinese textbooks have counted from the September 18th Incident of 1931 instead, calling it the "Fourteen Years' War of Resistance" (十四年抗戰).
  • "The war against Japan was fought mainly by one party."Both sides of the Strait have slanted the story. The Kuomintang's armies fought most of the large-scale battles at the front and suffered the heaviest losses; the Communist forces mainly fought a guerrilla war behind Japanese lines, tying down Japanese troops and expanding their bases. In the past each side stressed its own contribution; in recent years mainland China has also given more recognition to the role of the Kuomintang's armies.
  • "Life in the Republican era was all modern elegance."Only for a few people in a few cities. The qipao and dance halls of films and nostalgic works belonged mainly to the upper classes of big cities such as Shanghai. Most Chinese people lived in the countryside, where life was very hard, and they lived through war and famine.
How we know

Newspapers, photographs, diaries and oral history

Archives on both sides of the Strait

Much of the Nationalist Government's archive was taken to Taiwan in 1949 and is kept today by Academia Historica (國史館) in Taipei and other institutions; what stayed on the mainland is held by the Second Historical Archives of China (中國第二歷史檔案館) in Nanjing. Chiang Kai-shek's diaries, kept over several decades, were for many years held at the Hoover Institution at Stanford University in the United States, where they were open to researchers.

Newspapers and photographs

This was the first era in China with large numbers of newspapers, magazines, photographs and newsreels. Newspapers such as Shanghai's Shenbao (申報), along with the reports and photographs of foreign journalists, recorded war, famine and city life. Red Star Over China (《紅星照耀中國》), written by the American journalist Edgar Snow after he visited the Red Army's base in northern Shaanxi in 1936, introduced Mao Zedong to the West for the first time.

The voices of eyewitnesses

The evidence for the Nanjing Massacre includes survivors' testimony, the diaries of Japanese soldiers, letters written and film shot at the time by Western missionaries and businessmen, and the records of the postwar Tokyo Trial and Nanjing Trial (南京審判). John Rabe's diary was only published in the 1990s. The oral histories of many eyewitnesses were also recorded before they died.

Key terms

Terms from this era

Kuomintang / Communist Party (國民黨 / 共產黨)
The two most important political parties of the Republican era. They worked together twice and fought two civil wars.
Warlord (軍閥)
A general with a private army who controlled a territory of his own.
May Fourth Movement (五四運動)
The 1919 movement of students and the public against the decision of the Paris Peace Conference; also used more broadly for the new currents of thought of the time.
Vernacular Chinese (白話文)
Writing based on everyday speech, which replaced classical Chinese.
Long March (長征)
The Red Army's long retreat from Jiangxi to northern Shaanxi in 1934–1935.
War of Resistance against Japan (抗日戰爭)
China's war against the Japanese invasion, 1937–1945, or counted from 1931.
Republic calendar (民國紀年)
A way of numbering years with 1912 as year 1, still used in Taiwan today.
Liberation (解放)
The term used in mainland China for the Communist Party's coming to power in 1949. Taiwan does not use this word.
Hong Kong

Strikes, Japanese occupation and newcomers from the north

In 1925 British police in Shanghai opened fire on demonstrators, setting off a wave of anti-British protest across the country. In Hong Kong (香港), hundreds of thousands of workers went on strike and returned to Guangzhou. The strike lasted sixteen months and for a time brought Hong Kong's economy to a standstill. It is known as the (省港大罷工).

On 8 December 1941, a few hours after the attack on Pearl Harbor, Japanese forces attacked Hong Kong. British, Canadian, Indian and local Hong Kong defenders held out for eighteen days; on 25 December the Governor surrendered, a day remembered as "Black Christmas" (黑色聖誕). During the "three years and eight months" of Japanese occupation that followed, food was desperately short, and the Japanese forced large numbers of residents to leave, so that the population fell from about 1.6 million to about 600,000. The Hong Kong and Kowloon Brigade (港九大隊) of the Communist-led East River Column (東江縱隊) fought a guerrilla war in the New Territories (新界) and helped captured Allied personnel and cultural figures escape from Hong Kong. After Japan surrendered in August 1945, British rule was restored.

Around 1949 large numbers of people fled from the mainland to Hong Kong, among them textile families from Shanghai who brought their capital south. They laid the foundations for Hong Kong's postwar industrial take-off.

Meanwhile in Europe and the world

Between the two world wars

The First World War and the Chinese labourers

During the First World War, Britain and France recruited about 140,000 labourers from China to work behind the front lines in France and Belgium; the British called them the Chinese Labour Corps (華工軍團). Thousands of them died in Europe, and many are buried in the Chinese cemetery at Noyelles-sur-Mer in northern France. Their contribution was long forgotten, and only in recent years has Britain begun to commemorate it.

Revolution and depression

The Russian Revolution of 1917 directly influenced the founding of the Chinese Communist Party and the reorganisation of the Kuomintang. The Great Depression that began in 1929 swept the world. Japan was hit hard too; its military grew in power and the country turned to expansion abroad, invading northeast China in 1931.

A Scottish Olympic champion

Eric Liddell (李愛銳), the Scot who won the 400 metres at the 1924 Paris Olympics, was born in Tianjin (天津). After winning gold he went back to China as a missionary and teacher. During the Japanese occupation he was held in the Weixian (濰縣) internment camp in Shandong, where he died of illness in February 1945, only months before Japan surrendered. The film Chariots of Fire tells his story.

Places to visit

  • Sun Yat-sen Mausoleum and the Presidential Palace (中山陵與總統府)Nanjing, Jiangsu

    Sun Yat-sen's tomb, and the Republican-era Presidential Palace and offices of the Nationalist Government.

  • Memorial Hall of the Victims in Nanjing Massacre by Japanese Invaders (侵華日軍南京大屠殺遇難同胞紀念館)Nanjing, Jiangsu

    A memorial built on a site where victims' remains were excavated. John Rabe's former home is nearby.

  • Site of the First National Congress of the CCP (中共一大會址紀念館)Shanghai

    Where the Chinese Communist Party held its First National Congress in 1921, in what is now the Xintiandi area.

  • The Bund (外灘)Shanghai

    Republican-era banks, the customs house and hotels line the Huangpu River: the best place to get a feel for "modern Shanghai".

  • Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall and the National Palace Museum (國父紀念館與國立故宮博物院)Taipei

    A building commemorating Sun Yat-sen, and the great collection of treasures shipped from the mainland to Taiwan in 1949.

  • Stanley Military Cemetery and Hong Kong Museum of Coastal Defence (赤柱軍人墳場與香港海防博物館)Hong Kong

    The cemetery holds soldiers and civilians who died in the Battle of Hong Kong and during the Japanese occupation; the Museum of Coastal Defence tells the story of the Battle of Hong Kong in 1941.

  • Imperial War MuseumLondon, UK · Free

    Has exhibits and archives on the Chinese Labour Corps of the First World War and on the war in the Far East.

The one thing to rememberThe Republic of China was born when the emperor abdicated, but it went through warlord wars, the struggle between the Nationalists and the Communists, and Japanese invasion. In 1949 the Communists founded the People's Republic of China, while the government of the Republic of China withdrew to Taiwan, and the two sides of the Strait have been governed separately ever since.

Era 1

Prehistory and Legend

c. 7000 – 1600 BC

For thousands of years before writing appeared in China, people on this land were already farming, raising pigs, building villages, firing pottery and carving jade, and even building great walled cities with dams. China is one of the few places in the world where farming was invented independently, and it had two starting points: millet in the north and rice in the south. There are no written records from this period, so almost everything we know comes from archaeological digs and from legends handed down by later generations.

Before we begin

No writing: so how do we know?

Nobody wrote anything down in this period, so there are no written records at all. What we know comes from : archaeologists dig up the houses, graves, tools, pottery and rubbish that ancient people left in the ground, and then use scientific methods such as to work out how old they are. Archaeologists call this period the , or New Stone Age, meaning that people had learned to grind stone into smooth tools, and had begun to grow crops and settle down.

The other source is legend. More than a thousand years later, the Chinese wrote ancient stories that had been passed down by word of mouth into their history books. These stories matter, because they shaped how the Chinese saw their own ancestors, but they are not a reliable record of history.

Hundreds of thousands of years ago – c. 10,000 BC

The first people

Early humans were living in China hundreds of thousands of years ago. The most famous are (北京人), found at (周口店), southwest of Beijing (北京). They belonged to , an older kind of human than us; they could make chipped stone tools and may already have used fire. Today's Chinese are not direct descendants of Peking Man. Like people everywhere in the world, modern Chinese are mainly descended from modern humans who spread out of Africa tens of thousands of years ago.

Another group also lived in the caves at Zhoukoudian: the (山頂洞人), about thirty thousand years ago. They were already modern humans like us; they sewed animal-skin clothes with bone needles and wore necklaces made of animal teeth and shells. This was the Ice Age, and people lived by hunting and gathering wild fruit, moving from place to place with the seasons. Caves in Jiangxi (江西) and Hunan (湖南) have also produced some of the oldest pottery in the world, about twenty thousand years old, long before farming began.

c. 8000 – 5000 BC

Two kinds of farming: millet in the north, rice in the south

When the Ice Age ended and the climate grew warmer, people began to grow crops and to settle in one place for long periods. Farming in China had two starting points (see Map 1), and this had to do with the climate of each. In the north, the Yellow River (黃河) valley has cold winters and little rain, and people domesticated drought-hardy , along with broomcorn millet. In the south, the Yangtze (長江) valley is warm and wet, and there people domesticated . Rice husks about ten thousand years old found at (上山) in Zhejiang (浙江) are some of the earliest evidence of rice growing.

The first domestic animals were dogs and pigs, followed later by cattle, sheep and chickens. Even today, the northern Chinese taste for wheat foods such as noodles and the southern taste for rice can be traced back to these two ancient farming traditions.

The site of (賈湖) in Henan (河南) (about 7000 BC) is an example of an early farming village. It has produced made from the wing bones of red-crowned cranes, which can still play a musical scale: among the oldest playable musical instruments in the world. By analysing residues in pottery jars, researchers found that people here were already making an alcoholic drink from rice, honey and fruit such as hawthorn. Some tortoise shells are also carved with signs, but scholars still argue over whether these count as writing.

c. 5000 – 3000 BC

Village life

In 1921 the Swedish geologist (安特生) dug at a village called Yangshao (仰韶) in Henan and found large amounts of pottery painted with red and black patterns. Archaeologists therefore group this kind of site along the middle Yellow River under the name (仰韶文化), which was also the first prehistoric culture in China to be scientifically excavated.

The most famous Yangshao village is (半坡), near present-day Xi'an (西安). It was protected by a deep ditch around the outside, and inside were dozens of small round huts, half sunk into the ground, with roofs held up by wooden posts and covered in thatch and mud, and a hearth in the middle. Next to the village were kilns for firing pottery and a shared cemetery. A painted pottery basin found here, showing a human face with a fish on either side, is the best-known object of this period.

At the same time, on the damp coast of Zhejiang in the south, the people of (河姆渡) lived in wooden houses , which kept out both the damp and wild animals. They already knew how to join timber with (榫卯, sunmao), a nail-free woodworking technique later used in traditional Chinese buildings and furniture for thousands of years. Hemudu also produced a wooden bowl coated in red , one of the earliest pieces of lacquerware in the world.

c. 4500 – 3000 BC

Jade and dragons

Far away in the northeast, around Liaoning (遼寧) and Inner Mongolia (內蒙古), the (紅山文化) built stone altars and a "Goddess Temple" (女神廟, nüshen miao), where life-sized clay heads of women have been found. The Hongshan people were skilled at carving . The best known is a type of jade object with a head like a pig and a curled-up body, called a (玉豬龍, yuzhulong). Some think this is one of the earliest sources of the image of the Chinese dragon.

Jade held an extremely high place in Chinese culture. It is very hard, and ancient people had no metal tools, so they could only grind it slowly with sand and water; a single jade object could take months or even years. Jade was therefore a symbol of power and wealth, and later came to stand for noble character.

c. 3300 – 2300 BC

Liangzhu: a great city five thousand years ago

Five thousand years ago, in the area around today's Shanghai (上海) and Hangzhou (杭州), there was a powerful society known as the (良渚). Its centre was the ancient city of Liangzhu near Hangzhou. The area inside its walls was about three square kilometres, the size of more than four hundred football pitches, with city walls, a moat, and palaces built on man-made platforms.

Even more astonishing are the water works outside the city: the Liangzhu people piled up earth and bundles of grass to make a series of dams, creating large reservoirs used for flood control, irrigation and transport. This is one of the earliest large-scale water management systems known in the world, older even than the pyramids of Egypt.

The most important objects of the Liangzhu people were (玉琮): tube-shaped jades that are square outside and round inside, with a hole through the middle, finely carved with a "god and beast face" design. These jades are concentrated in a few large tombs, which shows that society had already split into a powerful elite and ordinary people. In 2019 the Archaeological Ruins of Liangzhu City (良渚古城遺址) were listed as a , and they are considered evidence that an early state had emerged in China five thousand years ago.

c. 2300 – 1600 BC

Towards the dynasties

After 3000 BC more and more large walled settlements appeared in different regions, and war became more and more frequent. At (陶寺) in Shanxi (山西) there was a large city with a semicircular structure that some scholars believe was an "observatory" for watching where the sun rose and setting the calendar. At (石峁) in northern Shaanxi (陝西) stood a huge stone-built fortress, with walls ten kilometres long.

Around 1900 BC a large centre unlike anything seen before appeared at (二里頭), near Luoyang (洛陽) in Henan: it had a palace area, a regular grid of roads, workshops making bronze, and a dragon-shaped object made from more than two thousand pieces of turquoise. Many scholars think Erlitou may have been the capital of the legendary (夏). The Xia is the first recorded in traditional Chinese histories: a dynasty being a regime ruled by one family, passed down from generation to generation. But no writing at all has been found at Erlitou, so whether the Xia really existed, and whether Erlitou was the Xia, is still not settled.

Legend

The Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors, and Yu tames the flood

Ancient Chinese histories record a set of legends about the distant past, known as the (三皇五帝, sanhuang wudi). In legend, the "Three Sovereigns" taught people to use fire, to hunt and to farm. The first of the "Five Emperors" was the (Huangdi, 黃帝), who defeated rival tribal chiefs and united the tribes, and was later honoured by the Chinese as their common ancestor. When Chinese people today call themselves "descendants of Yan and Huang" (炎黃子孫), the phrase comes from this legend.

Two of the Five Emperors, Yao (堯) and Shun (舜), are said not to have passed the throne to their own sons but to the most able and virtuous person. This is called (禪讓, shanrang), and later generations saw it as the ideal form of government.

The most famous legend is how (大禹) tamed the flood. In legend, floods were overrunning the land, and Yu's father tried for nine years to block the water with embankments, and failed. Yu instead cleared and deepened the river channels, leading the floodwater out to the sea. He worked on the floods for thirteen years, and it is said that he passed his own front door three times without going in. After Yu died, his son Qi (啟) inherited the throne, and from then on the throne passed down within one family from generation to generation: this was the legendary Xia dynasty.

Most of these stories were only written down more than a thousand years later, and cannot be treated as real history. But they reflect ancient ideas: the importance of controlling water, respect for virtuous leaders, and the idea that the throne is inherited. Their influence on Chinese culture has been profound.

Daily life

How people lived

Banpo village, c. 4500 BC

A deep ditch surrounds dozens of round huts, and each family eats and sleeps around the hearth in the middle of its hut. The villagers grow millet and keep pigs and dogs, and they also hunt, fish and gather wild fruit. Potters coil strips of clay round and round to build up pots, then paint patterns on them and fire them; craftsmen grind stone into axes and knives.

Hemudu by the water, c. 5000 BC

In the marshes by a lake, people live in houses raised on wooden stilts. They grow rice, gather water chestnuts and acorns, fish in the lake, and turn the soil with tools made from the shoulder blades of cattle. Archaeologists have found a thick layer of rice husks at the site, and a wooden paddle.

In the city of Liangzhu, c. 2800 BC

Craftsmen, priests and nobles live inside the city, and outside lie wide rice fields. Dams control the floods, rivers and canals criss-cross the land like a net, and people get about by bamboo raft and small boat. When nobles die they are buried with large numbers of jades, while the graves of ordinary people are much simpler.

Key people

Legendary figures and discoverers

  • The Yellow Emperor (黃帝)Legendary first of the Five Emperors

    The legendary chief who united the tribes, honoured by the Chinese as the "first ancestor of Chinese civilisation". Many inventions, such as clothing, carts and writing, are credited to him and his ministers.

  • Yu the Great (大禹)Legendary founder of the Xia dynasty

    Famous for taming the floods by clearing and channelling the rivers. His son Qi succeeded him, beginning, in legend, the tradition of passing the throne from father to son.

  • Johan Gunnar Andersson (安特生)Swedish geologist, 1874–1960

    Came to China to help search for minerals. In 1921 he led the dig at Yangshao village in Henan and discovered the Yangshao culture, which is seen as the beginning of modern archaeology in China.

  • Pei Wenzhong (裴文中)Archaeologist, 1904–1982

    In 1929 he found the first complete Peking Man skullcap at Zhoukoudian, astonishing the world. The original skulls went missing on their way to the United States during the Second World War, and have never been found.

  • Shi Xingeng (施昕更)Archaeologist, 1911–1939

    In 1936 he found black pottery in his home town of Liangzhu in Zhejiang, the first sign of the Liangzhu culture. He died of illness during the war against Japan (抗日戰爭), aged only twenty-eight.

  • Su Bingqi (蘇秉琦)Archaeologist, 1909–1997

    Put forward the "sky full of stars" theory of the origins of Chinese civilisation: cultures in different regions developed separately and influenced one another, like stars scattered across the sky, rather than spreading outwards from a single centre on the Yellow River.

Myth or fact

What people often get wrong

  • "China has five thousand years of history."It depends how you count. Recorded history, counting from the oracle bone inscriptions (甲骨文, jiaguwen) of the Shang dynasty (商朝) (see Era 2), is about 3,300 years old; if you count early states such as Liangzhu, it is more than five thousand. Both claims have a basis; they just use different standards.
  • "The Xia dynasty has been proved to exist."Not settled. Erlitou was very probably the capital of some early dynasty, but no written records of the Xia itself have yet been found. Most scholars in China believe the Xia existed; scholars elsewhere are more cautious.
  • "Chinese civilisation began only on the Yellow River."An outdated view. Liangzhu and Hemudu in the Yangtze valley, Hongshan in the northeast and other places each developed advanced cultures of their own. The Yellow River valley was just one centre among several.
  • "The Chinese are descended from Peking Man."Most scholars do not think so. Peking Man belonged to Homo erectus, an older kind of human. Genetic studies show that modern Chinese, like people everywhere in the world, are mainly descended from modern humans who spread out of Africa tens of thousands of years ago.
How we know

Evidence without writing

Archaeological digs

For this period we rely almost entirely on archaeology: houses, burials, pottery, jade and rubbish pits. Radiocarbon dating can measure how old charred seeds, bones and wood are, allowing different sites to be put in order.

Plant and animal remains

Archaeologists put soil in water so that tiny charred seeds float to the top, then identify them under a microscope as millet or rice. Studying animal bones tells us when pigs and dogs were domesticated, and DNA from ancient human bones shows how groups of people moved and mixed.

Later legends

The stories of the Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors and of the Xia appear in books such as the Book of Documents (尚書, Shangshu) and the Records of the Grand Historian (史記, Shiji) by Sima Qian (司馬遷) of the Han dynasty (漢朝), written more than a thousand years after the events they describe. Historians study them as ancient people's memories and ideas, not as direct evidence.

Key terms

Terms from this era

Neolithic (新石器時代)
The age of polished stone tools, when people began to grow crops and settle down. In China it began about ten thousand years ago.
Culture, in archaeology (文化)
Archaeologists call a group of sites with similar styles of objects a "culture", usually named after the place where it was first found, as with the Yangshao culture.
Painted pottery (彩陶)
Pottery painted with red, black and other patterns before firing; the signature object of the Yangshao culture.
Jade cong (玉琮)
A tube-shaped jade, square outside and round inside, used in rituals; the signature object of the Liangzhu culture.
Abdication to the worthy (禪讓)
In legend, handing the throne to the most able and virtuous person rather than to one's son.
Dynasty (王朝)
A regime ruled by one family, passed down from generation to generation, such as the Xia, Shang and Zhou (周).
Meanwhile in Europe and the world

Early civilisations around the world

The first writing

Around 3200 BC the Sumerians of Mesopotamia (today's Iraq) invented cuneiform, pressed into clay tablets, and hieroglyphs appeared in Egypt at about the same time. This was about two thousand years earlier than the oldest surviving mature writing in China.

The pyramids and Liangzhu

The Great Pyramid of Khufu in Egypt was built around 2560 BC, close to the height of the city of Liangzhu. Both needed huge amounts of labour and strong organisation, showing that there were already powers able to mobilise a whole society.

Far-off Scotland

While the Liangzhu people were building their dams, farmers in Orkney, off the north of Scotland, were building the stone village of Skara Brae and great stone circles. At both ends of Eurasia, complex farming societies appeared at almost the same time.

Places to visit

  • Liangzhu Archaeological Site Park and Liangzhu Museum (良渚古城遺址公園及良渚博物院)Hangzhou, Zhejiang

    A World Heritage Site. You can walk on the ancient city walls and see the remains of the dams; the museum has many jade cong and bi discs.

  • Xi'an Banpo Museum (西安半坡博物館)Xi'an, Shaanxi

    China's first museum built on an archaeological site, preserving the houses and ditch of a Yangshao culture village.

  • Erlitou Site Museum of the Xia Capital (二里頭夏都遺址博物館)Yanshi, Luoyang, Henan

    Shows Erlitou's palaces, bronzes and the turquoise dragon.

  • Peking Man Site at Zhoukoudian (周口店北京人遺址)Fangshan, Beijing

    A World Heritage Site, where Peking Man and the Upper Cave people were found.

  • British Museum, China galleryLondon · free

    Room 33 displays Chinese objects from the Neolithic to the present, including Liangzhu-style jade cong and Neolithic painted pottery.

  • National Museum of ScotlandEdinburgh · free

    The East Asia galleries have ancient Chinese pottery and jade, which you can compare with the Scottish prehistoric objects in the same museum.

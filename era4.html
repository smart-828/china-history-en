Era 4

Qin and Han

221 BC – AD 220

The First Emperor of Qin turned China, for the first time, into a single empire ruled directly by an emperor and his officials, and he standardised writing, money, and weights and measures. But the Qin dynasty lasted only fifteen years. The Han dynasty that followed kept the Qin system, softened it with Confucian ideas and ruled for more than four hundred years, linking China indirectly with Central Asia and even Rome through the Silk Road. The Han and the Roman Empire existed at the same time and were the two largest empires in the world. Today the Chinese call themselves "Han people", write "Han characters" and speak the "Han language": that word "Han" comes from this dynasty.

Before we begin

A short-lived empire and a long-lived one

This era covers two dynasties. The (秦) unified China in 221 BC and lasted only fifteen years; the (漢) lasted more than four hundred. In the middle, the Han throne was seized by a man called Wang Mang (王莽) and the dynasty was interrupted for over ten years, so it is divided into two halves: the first half, with its capital at Chang'an (長安, today's Xi'an 西安) in the west, is called the (西漢); the second half, with its capital at Luoyang (洛陽) in the east, is called the (東漢).

This era set the basic shape of China for the next two thousand years: one emperor ruling the whole country, officials appointed by the centre running the regions, one shared written script, and Confucian ideas as the official orthodoxy of the state. Although China later split apart many times, each time the aim was to reunite it, and one reason is that the Qin and Han left behind the idea that "all under heaven ought to be united".

221 – 210 BC

The First Emperor

After conquering the six other states, the King of Qin, (嬴政), felt that the title "king" was no longer good enough for him. He took one word each from the legendary "Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors" (三皇五帝) to create a new title, (皇帝), "emperor", and called himself Shi Huangdi (始皇帝), the "First Emperor", hoping his descendants would be called the Second, the Third, and so on for ten thousand generations. The title stayed in use until the last emperor abdicated in 1912.

Unlike the Zhou (周), the First Emperor did not hand out land to his relatives. Instead he divided the whole country into thirty-six (郡, jun), each divided into counties. The officials of commanderies and counties were appointed and dismissed by the emperor, could be moved at any time and could not pass their posts on to their sons. This was called the (郡縣制, junxian zhi). It was a state run by officials rather than by hereditary nobles, somewhat like the Roman practice at the same time of governing provinces through governors sent by the emperor; in later medieval Europe, by contrast, most regions were ruled by hereditary nobles.

To turn seven formerly separate states into one, the First Emperor brought in a series of unifying measures (see Figure 3). The whole country used one style of script, (小篆, xiaozhuan), a policy known as (書同文, shu tong wen). Standards of length, volume and weight were made uniform, rather like France bringing in the metric system at the end of the eighteenth century. The coins of the other states were abolished, and only one kind of round bronze coin with a square hole was used. The distance between the two wheels of a cart was fixed, so that vehicles could travel on roads all over the country. And great roads were built radiating out from the capital, Xianyang (咸陽), to every region, known as the (馳道, chidao). Of all these measures, the unified script had the deepest effect: the spoken dialects of different parts of China differ enormously, some so much that their speakers cannot understand each other, but the written characters are the same. This allowed China to keep a shared culture through its many later periods of division.

215 – 214 BC

A wall to the north, a war in the south

On the grasslands north of the Qin lived the (匈奴), nomads who lived by herding. They came and went on horseback like the wind, and often rode south to raid. The First Emperor sent his general (蒙恬) with an army of 300,000 to drive the Xiongnu back north of the Yellow River (黃河). He then joined up the walls that the states of Qin, Zhao (趙) and Yan (燕) had built in the north during the Warring States period (戰國), and extended them, forming a (長城, Changcheng) that ran from the northwest to Liaodong (遼東) in the northeast (see Map 2). Most of the Qin wall was made of rammed earth, quite different from the brick and stone Great Wall tourists see today. Meng Tian also built a military road about 700 km long, running straight from near Xianyang to the northern frontier, known as the (直道, zhidao).

In the south, in what are now Guangdong (廣東), Guangxi (廣西) and northern Vietnam, lived many different peoples, known together as the (百越). The First Emperor sent a large army south. To carry grain and supplies, he ordered the digging of a canal, the (靈渠), linking the Xiang River (湘江), which flows north, with the Li River (灕江), which flows south, so that boats could sail all the way from the Yangtze (長江) basin to the Pearl River (珠江) basin. The Lingqu still exists today. In 214 BC the Qin set up (南海郡) in Lingnan (嶺南, the lands south of the mountains), and the area of today's Guangzhou (廣州), Shenzhen (深圳) and Hong Kong (香港) became part of the territory of a Chinese dynasty for the first time (see "Hong Kong").

213 – 210 BC

Burning books, burying scholars, and an army underground

The Qin governed by the ideas of the Legalists (法家, fajia): its laws were harsh and it allowed no criticism. In 213 BC some Confucian scholars cited examples from ancient times to criticise the commandery-county system. The chancellor, (李斯), proposed that, apart from the Qin state's own histories and practical books on medicine, divination and farming, all privately held copies of the Book of Songs (詩經), the Book of Documents (尚書) and the histories of other states should be burned, and that anyone who dared to discuss the old books, or use the past to criticise the present, should be put to death. The next year, after some (方士) who were searching for an elixir of immortality for him had deceived him and fled, the First Emperor ordered more than four hundred fangshi and Confucian scholars in Xianyang to be buried alive. These two events are known together as the (焚書坑儒, fenshu kengru), and they made the First Emperor the model of a tyrant in the writings of later Confucians.

From the moment he came to the throne, the First Emperor used hundreds of thousands of workers to build his tomb at Mount Li (驪山). The tomb itself has still not been excavated, but in 1974 farmers digging a well nearby at Lintong (臨潼) in Shaanxi (陝西) found fragments of pottery soldiers: the world-famous (兵馬俑, bingmayong). Three pits are estimated to hold about eight thousand life-size pottery soldiers and horses, drawn up in neat battle formation. Every soldier has a different face and hairstyle, as if they were to go on guarding the emperor in the world after death.

210 – 207 BC

Why did the Qin fall so quickly?

In 210 BC the First Emperor fell ill and died while on a tour of his empire. The (趙高), who was travelling with him, and the chancellor Li Si kept his death secret, forged his last will, forced the emperor's eldest son to kill himself and put a younger son, Huhai (胡亥), on the throne as the "Second Emperor". Once he was enthroned, Zhao Gao controlled the court and killed ministers and members of the imperial family. He even had Li Si executed.

The ordinary people of the Qin were already crushed by their burdens: building the Great Wall, the tomb and palaces, and guarding the frontiers needed millions of labourers, and the laws were extremely harsh. In 209 BC a group of peasants called up to guard the northern frontier were held up by heavy rain and missed their deadline. According to the Records of the Grand Historian (史記, Shiji), under Qin law lateness was punished by beheading, so their leaders, (陳勝) and Wu Guang (吳廣), decided they might as well rebel. Chen Sheng's cry was, "Are kings, lords, generals and ministers born to it?" This was the first large-scale peasant uprising in Chinese history. Revolts broke out everywhere, and nobles of the six conquered states seized the chance to restore their kingdoms. In 207 BC the Qin dynasty fell, only fifteen years after unification.

People in the Han dynasty that followed debated again and again why the Qin had fallen. The most common conclusion was this: the Qin could win an empire with force and harsh punishment, but could not hold on to it by the same methods.

206 – 202 BC

Chu against Han: the noble and the commoner

Once the Qin had been overthrown, two leaders fought for the empire. One was (項羽), descended from a famous general of the state of Chu (楚), a fearless warrior so strong he could lift a bronze cauldron. The other was (劉邦), from a farming family, who had been only a minor official keeping order in a small rural district, but who was good at using talented people and knew how to listen to advice. Xiang Yu made Liu Bang King of Han, ruling Hanzhong (漢中) on the upper reaches of the Han River (漢水), and that is where the name "Han" comes from.

The two fought for four years, in what is known as the (楚漢相爭, Chu Han xiangzheng). The words "Chu River, Han Border" (楚河漢界) written across the middle of a Chinese chess board today are a reminder of this war. In 202 BC Xiang Yu was surrounded by the Han army at Gaixia (垓下). At night he heard Chu songs being sung by the Han troops all around him, and thought that his homeland of Chu had already fallen: the origin of the Chinese saying "Chu songs on all four sides" (四面楚歌), meaning to be surrounded by enemies. Xiang Yu broke out as far as the Wu River (烏江), but, feeling he could not face the elders of his homeland, cut his own throat with his sword. Liu Bang founded the Han dynasty with its capital at Chang'an. He was Emperor Gaozu of Han (漢高祖), the first emperor in Chinese history to come from a commoner's family.

202 – 141 BC

The early Han: letting the people rest

After years of war, the population of the early Han had fallen sharply; it is said that even the emperor could not find four horses of the same colour to pull his carriage. The Han kept the Qin's commandery-county system and most of its laws, but learned from the Qin's mistakes, greatly reducing taxes and forced labour to let the people recover. The court at the time favoured a school of thought called (黃老), which came from Daoism and believed in governing as little as possible. After several decades, by the reigns of Emperor Wen (漢文帝) and Emperor Jing (漢景帝), there was so much grain in the granaries that it rotted before it could be eaten. This period is known as the (文景之治, Wen Jing zhi zhi).

The Xiongnu in the north were still the greatest threat. Emperor Gaozu once led an army against them himself, but was surrounded by the Xiongnu for seven days at Mount Baideng (白登山) and was nearly captured. For more than sixty years afterwards, the Han had no choice but to follow a policy of (和親), "peace through marriage": princesses or women of the imperial clan were married to the chanyu (單于), the Xiongnu ruler, and large gifts of silk, wine and grain were sent every year in exchange for peace. Even so, the Xiongnu still often raided the south.

141 – 87 BC

Emperor Wu: Confucianism, salt and iron, and war

In 141 BC the sixteen-year-old (漢武帝) came to the throne, and he reigned for fifty-four years. He was one of the most accomplished, and most controversial, emperors in Chinese history.

In ideas, Emperor Wu accepted the advice of the Confucian scholar (董仲舒) and made Confucianism the official orthodoxy of the state, a policy known as (獨尊儒術, du zun rushu). He founded the (太學, taixue) in Chang'an, where students studied the Confucian classics and the best of them could become officials. From then on, studying the Confucian books became the main road to an official career, a tradition that lasted for two thousand years. But in practice the Han still relied heavily on Legalist laws and punishments, which later writers called "Confucian on the outside, Legalist on the inside".

In war, Emperor Wu abandoned the marriage policy and sent the young generals (衛青) and his nephew (霍去病) deep into the grasslands again and again. They defeated the Xiongnu heavily and took a long, narrow passage between high mountains and desert in what is now northwest Gansu (甘肅), known as the (河西走廊), where four commanderies were set up: Wuwei (武威), Zhangye (張掖), Jiuquan (酒泉) and Dunhuang (敦煌). This corridor later became the one route China had to pass through to reach the West. To pay for his huge military costs, Emperor Wu took the production and sale of salt and iron into state hands, known as the (鹽鐵專賣, yantie zhuanmai), and taxed merchants heavily. Year after year of war badly drained the population and the strength of the state, and in his old age Emperor Wu issued an edict admitting his own mistakes.

From 138 BC

Zhang Qian and the Silk Road

Emperor Wu heard that a people in the west called the Yuezhi (大月氏) had been defeated by the Xiongnu and had moved away westwards, and that they hated the Xiongnu. He wanted to ally with them and attack the Xiongnu from both sides. In 138 BC an official called (張騫) volunteered to go as envoy. Almost as soon as he set out he was captured by the Xiongnu and held for ten years, during which he married and had a son. Eventually he escaped, carried on westwards, crossed the Pamir mountains and at last found the Yuezhi. But by then the Yuezhi had settled happily in what is now northern Afghanistan and Uzbekistan and did not want to fight any more. On the way home Zhang Qian was captured by the Xiongnu again, and it was thirteen years in all before he returned to Chang'an. More than a hundred people had set out with him; only two came back.

Although Zhang Qian did not secure an alliance, he brought back the first first-hand information about the countries of Central Asia: they had cities and markets, and a fine breed of horse called the (汗血馬, hanxuema). The Han soon sent out embassies and trading caravans and established links with the states of Central Asia. From then on a trade network gradually took shape, linking China, Central Asia, India, Iran and even the Mediterranean (see Map 1). Chinese silk travelled west, as far as Rome; grapes, alfalfa, walnuts and pomegranates came into China. This network was later called the (絲綢之路, sichou zhi lu), but the name was only invented in the nineteenth century by a German geographer. Note that very few merchants travelled the whole way: goods were passed along in relays, from one group of merchants to the next.

c. 104 – 91 BC

Sima Qian and the Records of the Grand Historian

(司馬遷) was the official at Emperor Wu's court responsible for recording astronomy and history. On his deathbed, his father asked him to write a history from ancient times to the present. In 99 BC a general called Li Ling (李陵) was defeated and surrendered to the Xiongnu. The ministers at court all condemned him; only Sima Qian spoke up in his defence. This enraged Emperor Wu, and Sima Qian was sentenced to . A scholar who suffered this punishment would normally prefer to kill himself, but Sima Qian endured the shame and lived on in order to finish his work.

The work he completed, the , has 130 chapters and covers about three thousand years of history, from the legendary Yellow Emperor (黃帝) to Emperor Wu. It is built mainly around biographies: emperors, generals, ministers, assassins, merchants and knights-errant all have chapters of their own. It is vividly written, and even the loser Xiang Yu is portrayed with great feeling. Every later dynasty's official history, the so-called (正史, zhengshi), followed its model. Many of the stories we read in Eras 2 and 3 come from this book.

AD 9 – 25

Wang Mang: a failed reform

In the late Western Han, most emperors were young or weak, and the court was controlled by the families of empresses and empresses dowager. These relatives by marriage were called (外戚, waiqi). One of them, , won great respect for his frugal life and his devotion to Confucian learning. In AD 9 he accepted the "abdication" of the Han emperor, became emperor himself and founded the Xin (新, "New") dynasty.

Wang Mang tried to remake society according to the Zhou system described in ancient Confucian books: he took land into state ownership to share it out equally, banned the buying and selling of slaves, and changed the currency several times. These reforms were out of touch with reality and caused enormous chaos. On top of this the Yellow River burst its banks and changed course, famine spread, and uprisings broke out everywhere. In AD 23 Wang Mang was killed in Chang'an. Some modern writers have called him "a socialist two thousand years ago", but the comparison is not accurate.

AD 25 – 189

The Eastern Han: paper, an earthquake detector and Buddhism

In AD 25 (劉秀), a descendant of the Han imperial family, re-established the Han dynasty with its capital at Luoyang. He was Emperor Guangwu of Han (漢光武帝). The Eastern Han general (班超) spent thirty years in the Western Regions (西域), reopening the Silk Road. In AD 97 he sent his officer Gan Ying (甘英) as envoy to (大秦), the Roman Empire. Gan Ying reached the region of the Persian Gulf, where local people told him that the sea crossing would take two years, and he turned back. And so the Han and Rome missed their chance to meet face to face.

The Eastern Han was also an important age of invention. In AD 105 the eunuch (蔡倫) improved the method of making paper, producing cheap and practical paper from tree bark, hemp waste, old rags and worn-out fishing nets, and presented it to the emperor. Paper later travelled through Central Asia and the Arab world, and only reached Europe in the twelfth century. The scientist (張衡) built an (候風地動儀, houfeng didongyi) which, it is said, could sense distant earthquakes and show which direction they came from. Around the beginning of the Eastern Han, (佛教, fojiao) came into China from India by way of Central Asia, and China's first Buddhist temple, the White Horse Temple (白馬寺), was built in Luoyang. Over the following centuries Buddhism changed China profoundly.

AD 168 – 220

The end of the Han

In the late Eastern Han, most emperors came to the throne as boys and died before they reached middle age. The consort kin and the eunuchs took turns to control the court, killing each other. In the provinces, rich and powerful great families swallowed up more and more land, and more and more peasants lost their fields and became tenants or wanderers. In AD 184 a man called Zhang Jiao (張角) organised the people through a religious movement and launched a huge uprising. Its members wrapped yellow cloths around their heads, and it is known as the (黃巾之亂).

To crush the rebellion, the court let local officials and powerful families raise their own troops. From then on these men kept their armies for themselves and became warlords, each controlling his own territory. The strongest of them was (曹操), who held the last Han emperor, Emperor Xian (漢獻帝), in his power, "holding the Son of Heaven hostage to command the lords". In AD 220, after Cao Cao died, his son Cao Pi (曹丕) forced Emperor Xian to give up the throne. The Han dynasty formally came to an end, and China entered the Three Kingdoms period (三國; see Era 5).

Daily life

How people lived

A Han farming family

A typical farming household was a family of five, working about a hundred mu (about five hectares in today's measure) of land. The Han land tax was very light, sometimes only one-thirtieth of the harvest, but farmers also had to pay a poll tax and do forced labour every year, and adult men had to do military service. When natural disasters struck, many were forced to sell their land to big landlords and became tenants.

The lady of Mawangdui

In 1972, at Mawangdui (馬王堆) in Changsha (長沙), Hunan (湖南), archaeologists uncovered the body of a Western Han noblewoman, Xin Zhui (辛追), the wife of the chancellor of a local kingdom. More than two thousand years after her death her skin was still supple and her joints could still move. Her tomb held lacquerware, musical instruments, bamboo slips recording recipes, and a silk robe as thin as a cicada's wing that weighs only about 49 grams, showing the refined life of the Han nobility.

Inside Chang'an

Western Han Chang'an was one of the largest cities in the world at the time, with walls about 25 km round. It had an East Market and a West Market, with shops arranged by trade and opening and closing at fixed times. The city was home to officials, merchants, craftsmen and traders from the Western Regions. Han people loved the "hundred entertainments": acrobatics, wrestling, conjuring, song and dance, which often appear in the stone carvings found in tombs.

Key people

Who's who

  • Qin Shi Huang (秦始皇, Ying Zheng)The First Emperor, 259–210 BC

    Conquered the six other states and created the imperial system of rule by an emperor and his officials. Later generations are sharply divided over him: he is both the man who laid the foundations of a united China and the model of a tyrant.

  • Liu Bang (劉邦, Emperor Gaozu of Han)Founder of the Han, 256 or 247–195 BC

    A minor official from a farming family who was good at using talented people. He defeated Xiang Yu and founded the Han dynasty, the first emperor of China to come from a commoner's family.

  • Xiang Yu (項羽)Chu nobleman and general, 232–202 BC

    The main force in overthrowing the Qin, a fearless warrior who still lost to Liu Bang. His tragic end is one of the favourite subjects of Chinese drama and poetry; the Peking opera Farewell My Concubine (霸王別姬) tells his story.

  • Emperor Wu of Han (漢武帝, Liu Che)Emperor, 156–87 BC

    Reigned for fifty-four years. He made Confucianism the orthodoxy, struck north against the Xiongnu and opened up the Western Regions, but his constant wars badly drained the state.

  • Zhang Qian (張騫)Diplomat, died 114 BC

    Went twice as envoy to the Western Regions, and is remembered as the man who opened the way west; the Silk Road grew from his journeys.

  • Sima Qian (司馬遷)Historian, c. 145 – c. 86 BC

    Completed the Records of the Grand Historian even after being castrated. He is called the father of Chinese history and is often compared with the ancient Greek historian Herodotus.

  • Ban Zhao (班昭)Historian, c. AD 45–117

    The younger sister of Ban Chao and Ban Gu (班固). After her brother Ban Gu died she finished his History of the Han (漢書). She was China's first woman historian, and often went to the palace to teach the empress and the women of the court.

Myth or fact

What people often get wrong

  • "The Great Wall was built by the First Emperor."Only partly true. The Warring States had already built walls, and the First Emperor joined them up and extended them. The brick and stone Great Wall tourists see today was mostly built by the Ming (明) dynasty more than a thousand years later, and in a different place from the Qin wall (see Era 7).
  • "You can see the Great Wall from space with the naked eye."False. The Great Wall is very long but very narrow, and its colour is close to the ground around it. China's first astronaut, Yang Liwei (楊利偉), also said he could not see it from space.
  • "The burning of the books destroyed every book, and every Confucian scholar was killed."Exaggerated. The Qin burned privately held poetry and history books and the histories of other states; books on medicine, divination and farming were spared, and the court's own scholars kept their libraries. Of the more than four hundred people buried alive, many were fangshi rather than Confucian scholars.
  • "Chen Sheng and Wu Guang rebelled because Qin law said that anyone late would be beheaded."Doubtful. This story comes from the Records of the Grand Historian. But Qin legal texts on bamboo slips, excavated in 1975, show that the punishment for arriving late for labour service was only a fine, even for a delay of more than ten days. Chen Sheng may have exaggerated the danger to persuade his companions to rebel.
  • "Paper was invented by Cai Lun."Not exactly. Archaeologists have found paper in Western Han tombs and sites, one or two hundred years older than Cai Lun, and one piece even has a map drawn on it. Cai Lun's contribution was to improve the materials and method, making paper cheap and practical, and to have it promoted by the court.
  • "The Terracotta Army was always grey."No. The figures were originally painted in bright colours. Once excavated and exposed to the air, the paint curls and flakes off within minutes. This is one reason why the tomb and some of the pits have still not been excavated.
How we know

Histories, bamboo slips and tombs

The Shiji and the History of the Han

Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian and the History of the Han (漢書) by Ban Gu and Ban Zhao are the main histories for understanding the Qin and Han. The History of the Han records a registered population of about 59.6 million for the whole country in AD 2, one of the earliest complete national censuses in the world to survive.

Bamboo and wooden slips

Qin bamboo slips excavated at Shuihudi (睡虎地) in Hubei (湖北) in 1975 preserve a large number of Qin laws. More than thirty thousand Qin slips found in 2002 in an old well at Liye (里耶) in Hunan are the official files of a Qin county government. Han slips found at frontier forts in the northwest record the daily life of soldiers guarding the frontier, right down to requests for leave and records of debts.

A world in the tombs

The Terracotta Army, the Han tombs at Mawangdui and the tomb of the King of Nanyue (南越王) in Guangzhou let us see directly how people of the Qin and Han dressed, ate, lived and travelled. Han people believed that life after death was much like life before it, so tombs often held pottery models of houses, granaries, pigsties, wells and even musicians and dancers, like a world in miniature.

Key terms

Terms from this era

Emperor (皇帝)
The title created by the First Emperor, drawn from the "Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors", and used until 1912.
Commandery-county system (郡縣制)
A system in which the centre sent officials to govern the regions and posts could not be inherited. It replaced the Zhou system of granting fiefs.
Same script (書同文)
The policy of unifying writing across the country, with small seal script as the standard.
Honouring Confucianism alone (獨尊儒術)
The establishment of Confucianism as the official orthodoxy of the state under Emperor Wu of Han.
Heqin (和親)
The policy of marrying women of the imperial family to foreign rulers in exchange for peace.
Western Regions (西域)
The Han name for the lands west of the Yumen (玉門關) and Yang (陽關) passes, mainly today's Xinjiang (新疆) and Central Asia.
Silk Road (絲綢之路)
The network of overland trade routes linking China with Central Asia, Western Asia and the Mediterranean. The name only dates from the nineteenth century.
Han people (漢人)
Originally the people of the Han dynasty; later the name of China's largest ethnic group.
Hong Kong

Hong Kong becomes part of China for the first time

In 214 BC the Qin set up Nanhai Commandery in Lingnan, with its seat at Panyu (番禺, today's Guangzhou). The area of today's Hong Kong came under Panyu County: the first time Hong Kong became part of the territory of a Chinese dynasty. When the Qin fell, the military commander of Nanhai, (趙佗), took advantage of the chaos to make himself king and founded the (南越國), which ruled Lingnan for nearly a hundred years until it was destroyed by Emperor Wu of Han in 111 BC.

In 1955, while the government was cutting into a hillside at Lei Cheng Uk (李鄭屋) village in Sham Shui Po (深水埗) to build resettlement blocks, workers discovered an Eastern Han brick tomb. Its bricks bear inscriptions such as "Great luck to Panyu" (大吉番禺) and "Panyu, well governed" (番禺大治曆), showing that Hong Kong was under Panyu County in the Eastern Han. They are among the earliest physical evidence of Hong Kong's links with the Chinese empire. The tomb is now the Lei Cheng Uk Han Tomb Museum, and entry is free.

Meanwhile in Europe and the world

Two great empires, east and west

Rome and the Han

The Han and the Roman Empire were the two largest empires in the world at the time, each with a population of around fifty to sixty million (see Figure 4). Julius Caesar was assassinated in 44 BC, and in 27 BC Octavian became the first Roman emperor, during the late Western Han. Each empire knew the other existed, but they never had formal direct contact.

Romans loved silk

Chinese silk reached Rome through merchants of Central Asia and Parthia (安息, today's Iran), and became an expensive luxury. The Roman scholar Pliny the Elder complained that huge sums of money flowed east every year to buy silk and spices. The History of the Later Han (後漢書) records that in AD 166 envoys claiming to have been sent by "Andun" (安敦), king of Rome, reached the Han. "Andun" was very probably the Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius Antoninus, but the envoys may simply have been merchants.

Scotland's two walls

The Romans invaded Britain in AD 43, and in the 80s AD a Roman army pushed deep into what is now Scotland. In AD 122 the emperor Hadrian ordered a wall to be built across northern Britain; twenty years later, further north, the Romans built the Antonine Wall right across central Scotland, from the Forth to the Clyde. At the same time, the Eastern Han was defending its own northern frontier against the Xiongnu and the Xianbei (鮮卑).

  • Han Yangling Museum (漢陽陵博物館)Xi'an, Shaanxi

    The tomb of Emperor Jing of Han. Its pits hold thousands of smaller-than-life pottery figures, which you can look down on through a glass floor.

  • Shaanxi History Museum (陝西歷史博物館)Xi'an, Shaanxi

    A treasure house of Zhou, Qin, Han and Tang objects, and the best place to start learning about the history of Chang'an.

  • Hunan Museum (湖南博物院)Changsha, Hunan

    All the finds from the Han tombs at Mawangdui, including Xin Zhui's body and the silk robe as thin as a cicada's wing.

  • Museum of the Nanyue King (南越王博物院)Guangzhou, Guangdong

    The tomb of the second king of Nanyue, where a "jade suit sewn with silk thread", made of more than two thousand jade plaques, was found.

  • Lei Cheng Uk Han Tomb Museum (李鄭屋漢墓博物館)Sham Shui Po, Hong Kong · free

    Hong Kong's only Han dynasty tomb. You can see inside the burial chamber through glass.

  • British Museum, China galleryLondon · free

    Han pottery model houses, lacquerware, bronze mirrors and coins.

The one thing to rememberThe Qin created a unified empire ruled by an emperor and his officials; the Han secured it with Confucian ideas and linked China to the world through the Silk Road, and "Han" has been the Chinese people's name for themselves ever since.

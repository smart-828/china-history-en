Era 6

Song and Yuan

907 – 1368

After the fall of the Tang (唐), the Song (宋) dynasty reunited most of China, but in the north it always had powerful neighbours: the Liao (遼) of the Khitan (契丹) people, the Western Xia (西夏) of the Tangut (黨項) people and the Jin (金) of the Jurchen (女真) people. Militarily the Song was under constant threat, yet its economy and culture flourished as never before. Cities bustled all night, the world's first government paper money appeared here, movable type printing, gunpowder and the navigational compass were all invented or first recorded in this period, and the civil service examination (科舉, keju) let the sons of ordinary families become officials through study. In the end, Genghis Khan (成吉思汗) from the Mongolian steppe and his descendants built a vast empire stretching across Europe and Asia, destroyed the Southern Song (南宋) and founded the Yuan dynasty (元朝). For the first time, all of China was ruled by a people from the north.

Before we begin

A China that did not rule alone

This era lasts about 460 years and falls into four parts:

  • (五代十國; 907–979): more than half a century of division after the fall of the Tang.
  • (北宋; 960–1127): the capital was Kaifeng (開封), on the Yellow River (黃河). To the north lay the Liao, to the northwest the Western Xia.
  • (1127–1279): after the Jin state of the Jurchen people took the north, the Song retreated south, with its capital at Hangzhou (杭州).
  • (1271–1368): the Mongols united all of China, with their capital at today's Beijing (北京).

Unlike the Han (漢) and the Tang, the Song never ruled the whole of what is now northern China. It was only one of several strong states in East Asia (see Map 1). The Liao state founded by the (契丹) people in the north was so powerful that Central Asians and Europeans used the name "Khitan" for the whole of China: the "Cathay" of medieval Europeans, and the Russian word for China today, Китай (Kitai), both come from it.

907 – 979

The Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms, and the founding of the Song

After the fall of the Tang, the north went through five short-lived dynasties in a little over fifty years, while the south split into more than ten rival kingdoms. This is known as the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms. Most of the emperors of this time were soldiers, and mutinies that put a new man on the throne were an everyday event. As one saying put it: "The Son of Heaven is simply whoever has the strongest troops and the sturdiest horses."

In 960 (趙匡胤), commander of the palace army of the Later Zhou (後周) dynasty, led his troops out on campaign. At Chenqiao (陳橋), a staging post near Kaifeng, his officers threw an emperor's yellow robe over his shoulders and proclaimed him emperor, an event remembered as the "Chenqiao Mutiny" (陳橋兵變) or "putting on the yellow robe" (黃袍加身). He became Emperor Taizu of Song (宋太祖). Knowing exactly how he had come to power, after taking the throne he held a banquet for the veteran generals who commanded the armies and, over the wine, persuaded them to give up their commands and retire to a comfortable life at home. This is known as . From then on the Song followed the principle of : the state was run by civil officials, not generals, the army was commanded by civil officials, and generals were moved around often. As a result, in more than three hundred years of Song rule no soldier ever seized the throne, but the army's fighting strength was weakened.

936 – 1044

The Liao, the Western Xia and a peace paid for with money

From the start the Song faced a hard problem. During the Five Dynasties, a warlord in the north China plain, bidding for the throne, had handed sixteen prefectures around today's Beijing and Datong (大同) to the Khitan. These were called the . The area included the passes along the line of the Great Wall (長城); without it, the north China plain had no natural defences, and horsemen from the north could ride straight in. The Song tried twice to win it back, and both times the Liao army defeated them.

In 1004 the Liao army invaded in force and reached Chanzhou (澶州, today's Puyang 濮陽 in Henan 河南) on the Yellow River, little more than a hundred kilometres from the Song capital at Kaifeng. Urged on by his minister Kou Zhun (寇準), Emperor Zhenzong (宋真宗) went to the front in person. Neither side could win, and in 1005 they agreed the : every year the Song would send the Liao 100,000 taels of silver and 200,000 bolts of silk, and the two states would address each other as brothers. Peace then lasted for more than a hundred years. Later the Song made peace in a similar way with the (西夏) in the northwest. Many people have criticised this as a humiliating "peace bought with money", but these annual payments were only a small part of the Song's income, far cheaper than war, and much of the money flowed back to the Song through trade on the border.

960 – 1127

The Northern Song: thriving cities and trade

The Song was one of the most economically advanced periods in Chinese history. (占城稻, zhancheng), a drought-resistant, fast-ripening variety brought in from Vietnam, allowed many places south of the Yangtze (長江) to grow two harvests a year. Food supplies rose sharply, and in the early twelfth century the population passed one hundred million for the first time, more than the population of the whole of Europe.

The Northern Song capital, (開封), may have had a million people. The Tang system, as in Chang'an (長安), of walling in residential wards and locking their gates at night was broken down. Shops could open along the streets, night markets traded until dawn, and in restaurants, teahouses and entertainment quarters called there were storytellers, acrobats, puppet shows and all kinds of performances. A long scroll by the painter (張擇端), (清明上河圖), shows the bustle inside and outside Kaifeng. It contains more than eight hundred people, along with boats, bridges, shops, carts and horses, in detail as rich as a photograph.

Trade grew so much that there were not enough copper coins. Merchants in Sichuan (四川) were the first to issue paper certificates that could be exchanged for cash, called (交子). In 1024 the Song government took over issuing them: the world's first paper money issued by a government. Europe did not have banknotes, issued by banks, until the seventeenth century.

960 – 1279

A world run by scholars

Under the Tang, the was only one way into government; the Song made it the main way. Each Song examination passed far more candidates for the highest degree (jinshi, 進士) than the Tang did, and the examinations aimed to be fair: candidates' names were pasted over, and every paper was recopied by clerks so that examiners could not recognise the handwriting. The final stage was overseen by the emperor himself, and was called the "palace examination" (殿試, dianshi). A child from a poor farming family could become chief minister if he studied and passed. A widely known Song poem says: "In the morning a lad in a farmhouse, by evening in the hall of the Son of Heaven."

The scholars who became officials through the examinations were called . They were officials, but also scholars, poets and calligraphers, and saw themselves as responsible for the state and for the world. The great minister (范仲淹) wrote that a scholar should be "the first to worry about the world's troubles, and the last to enjoy its pleasures". As printing spread, books became cheap, academies and schools were opened everywhere, and the number of people who could read grew enormously.

The scholar-official best loved by later generations is (蘇軾), also known as Su Dongpo (蘇東坡). He was a poet, a writer of song lyrics, an essayist, a calligrapher and a painter, and also a lover of good food: the famous "Dongpo pork" (東坡肉) is said to be connected with him. He was arrested and imprisoned for writing poems that criticised government policy, and late in life he was banished to Huizhou (惠州) in the far south and then to distant Hainan (海南) Island, yet he always kept his optimism and sense of humour.

1069 – 1085

Wang Anshi's reforms: a great debate

By the middle of the Northern Song, the court was paying for a huge army and civil service, and its finances were getting harder and harder. In 1069 Emperor Shenzong (宋神宗) appointed (王安石) to carry out reforms, known as (王安石變法). The most famous was the "Green Shoots Law" (青苗法, qingmiaofa): in the lean months before the harvest, the government lent money to farmers at low interest, to be repaid after the harvest, so that they would not have to borrow from moneylenders. Other measures let people pay money instead of doing labour service, organised village militias and had the government steady prices.

The reforms were fiercely opposed by a group of ministers led by the historian (司馬光). They argued that the new laws harmed the people: local officials, eager to show results, forced farmers to borrow, which made their burden heavier, not lighter. The officials who supported and opposed the reforms split into two factions that attacked each other, and court policy swung back and forth for decades, in what is known as the factional struggle between the New and Old parties (新舊黨爭). The argument was a little like today's debates about how far government should intervene in the economy. After Sima Guang was pushed out of court, he spent nineteen years compiling a chronicle running from the Warring States (戰國) to the Five Dynasties, the Comprehensive Mirror for Aid in Government (資治通鑑, Zizhi tongjian), which is still one of China's most important works of history.

c. 1040 – 1120

Inventions that changed the world

In 1620 the English philosopher Francis Bacon wrote that three inventions, printing, gunpowder and the compass, had "changed the whole face and state of things throughout the world". He did not know that the earliest reliable records of all three come from the Song (see Figure 2).

  • Movable type printing: woodblock printing already existed under the Tang. In about the 1040s a commoner named (畢昇) made individual characters out of baked clay, which could be set up to print a page and then taken apart and reused. But Chinese has several thousand common characters, so in China movable type never became as widespread as woodblocks. In Europe, Gutenberg invented metal movable type in about 1450, and because the alphabet has only twenty-odd letters, it had a much bigger impact.
  • Gunpowder: Tang alchemists trying to make an elixir of immortality found that a mixture of saltpetre, sulphur and charcoal would burn and explode. In 1044 an official Song military manual, the (武經總要, Wujing zongyao), recorded three gunpowder formulas, the earliest written gunpowder formulas in the world. The Song already used fire arrows, fire bombs and fire lances shot from bamboo tubes.
  • The navigational compass: the Chinese had long known that a lodestone points south, but the earliest clear record of using a compass to navigate at sea is in a book of notes written in about 1117, which describes seagoing ships at Guangzhou (廣州) "watching the south-pointing needle" to find their way on cloudy days.

Bi Sheng's invention was recorded by the Northern Song scientist (沈括) in his Dream Pool Essays (夢溪筆談, Mengxi bitan). The book also notes that a magnetic needle does not point due south but slightly off it, one of the earliest records anywhere of magnetic declination.

1100 – 1127

The artist emperor and the Jingkang Incident

(宋徽宗) was an extraordinarily gifted artist. His paintings of flowers and birds and the "slender gold" (瘦金體, shoujin ti) style of calligraphy he invented are still treasured today. But he was a very poor emperor. Absorbed in art and Daoism (道教), he had rare plants and strange-shaped rocks collected from the lower Yangtze region and shipped to Kaifeng by fleets of boats to build gardens in the palace, wearing out the people and wasting money, and sparking revolts. The stories of the outlaw heroes of Liangshan (梁山) in (水滸傳) are set in this period.

Meanwhile, in the northeast, the (女真) people rose to power and founded the (金) state. The Song hoped to use the Jin to win back the Sixteen Prefectures, so it allied with the Jin to attack the Liao from both sides. The Jin destroyed the Liao, but the Song army performed very badly, and the Jin, having seen how weak the Song was, turned south to invade it. In 1127 the Jin army took Kaifeng and carried off to the north Huizong, who had already abdicated, his son Emperor Qinzong (宋欽宗), and more than ten thousand members of the imperial family, consorts, ministers and craftsmen, together with huge quantities of treasure, books and ritual objects. It is one of the most humiliating events in Chinese history, and is known by the reign title of the time as the . Huizong later died in the northeast and never came home.

1127 – 1142

The Southern Song and Yue Fei

Another of Huizong's sons, Zhao Gou (趙構), escaped south and took the throne as Emperor Gaozong (宋高宗). He later made his capital at Lin'an (臨安, today's Hangzhou), and this is known as the Southern Song. At one point the Jin army crossed the Yangtze and chased Gaozong out to sea. Of the generals who fought the Jin, the most famous was (岳飛). Born into a farming family, he ran his army with strict discipline ("we would freeze to death rather than tear down houses for firewood, and starve rather than plunder"), beat the Jin army again and again, at one point recovered a large area around Luoyang (洛陽), and was preparing to march on Kaifeng.

But Emperor Gaozong and his chief minister (秦檜) were set on making peace. In a single day Gaozong sent twelve gold-lacquered tablets (十二道金牌) ordering Yue Fei to return. In 1142 Qin Hui had Yue Fei put to death on a charge described as moxuyou (莫須有), meaning "perhaps there was" something. In the same year the Song signed a treaty with the Jin, fixing the border along the line from the Huai River (淮河) to the Qinling mountains (秦嶺), and accepting the status of a vassal paying tribute every year (see Map 1). Yue Fei later became China's most revered national hero, and Qin Hui's name became a byword for a treacherous minister. At the Temple of Yue Fei (岳王廟) in Hangzhou, iron statues of Qin Hui and his wife, kneeling with their hands tied behind their backs before Yue Fei's tomb, are still there today.

Why would an emperor recall a general who was winning? Besides fearing that Yue Fei's success made him a threat to the throne, some people believe Gaozong also worried that if the Jin were defeated, his captive elder brother Qinzong would be sent back and threaten his own position as emperor.

1127 – 1279

The Southern Song: facing the sea

After losing the north, the Southern Song's economic centre moved entirely south of the Yangtze. Lin'an became one of the largest and richest cities in the world. With the overland Silk Road (絲綢之路) blocked by the states to the north, the Southern Song threw its energy into sea trade, setting up (市舶司, shibosi) in Guangzhou, Quanzhou (泉州) and other ports to oversee foreign merchant ships and collect customs duties, which became an important source of government income. (泉州) in Fujian (福建) was home to large numbers of Arab and Persian merchants, and the city had mosques, a Hindu temple and gravestones of Nestorian Christians. Marco Polo and the Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta later called it one of the greatest ports in the world. Chinese porcelain, silk and tea were shipped from here to Southeast Asia, India, Arabia and East Africa. The Southern Song shipwreck , found off the coast of Guangdong (廣東) in 1987, carried more than a hundred thousand pieces of porcelain and other goods.

In the world of ideas, (朱熹) of the Southern Song brought together the teachings of Song Confucian scholars into a new form of Confucianism called (理學), known in the West as Neo-Confucianism. It joined Confucian morality to the "principle" (li) found in everything in the universe. Zhu Xi's commentaries on the Analects (論語), the Mencius (孟子), the Great Learning (大學) and the Doctrine of the Mean (中庸), together called the , became the standard texts for the civil service examination from the Yuan dynasty onwards, and shaped China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam for six or seven hundred years.

1206 – 1279

The Mongol conquest

In 1206 Temujin (鐵木真), a tribal chief on the Mongolian steppe, united the Mongol tribes and was given the title . The Mongols were nomadic herders, only a little over a million people, but every man had grown up in the saddle and was a horseman. They were disciplined, fast-moving, and good at taking in the technology and talent of the peoples they conquered. Within half a century, Genghis Khan and his descendants built the largest continuous land empire in human history, stretching from the Korean peninsula to today's Russia, Iran and Eastern Europe (see Map 3).

The Mongols destroyed first the Western Xia and then the Jin. The Southern Song had joined the Mongols to destroy the Jin, but immediately became the Mongols' next target. Protected by the Yangtze and its many walled cities, the Southern Song held out stubbornly for more than forty years, longer than any other state the Mongols attacked. Xiangyang (襄陽), a key stronghold on the Han River (漢水), a tributary of the Yangtze, was besieged for nearly six years, until the Mongols broke its walls with giant catapults built by Persian engineers. In 1276 Lin'an surrendered.

What was left of the Southern Song court fled south with a boy emperor along the coasts of Fujian and Guangdong, and stopped for a while around today's Hong Kong (香港; see "Hong Kong"). In 1279 the Song fleet was wiped out at the sea battle of Yashan (崖山), at Xinhui (新會) in Guangdong, and the minister Lu Xiufu (陸秀夫) jumped into the sea with the seven-year-old emperor on his back. The Song chief minister (文天祥) was captured and refused to surrender. In prison he wrote the Song of Righteousness (正氣歌), and four years later he went calmly to his execution. In a poem he wrote while being taken as a prisoner across the Lingding Sea (零丁洋), outside the mouth of the Pearl River (珠江), are two lines that are among the best known in Chinese: "Since ancient times, who has lived and not died? Let me leave a loyal heart to shine in the pages of history."

1260 – 1368

The Yuan: China under Mongol rule

In 1271 Genghis Khan's grandson (忽必烈) adopted a Chinese-style dynastic name, "Great Yuan" (大元), and built his capital, (大都), on the site of today's Beijing. From then on Beijing has been China's capital right up to the present (with only brief exceptions). Kublai also twice sent great armies across the sea to invade Japan; both failed because of typhoons and Japanese resistance, and the Japanese called the typhoons kamikaze, "divine wind".

The Yuan divided the people roughly into four classes: . The high offices were held mainly by Mongols and semu. The civil service examination was suspended for decades and only restored in 1315, with very few places. Many scholars, with no way into government, turned to writing plays, so the Yuan became the golden age of Chinese drama, known as (元曲); Guan Hanqing's (關漢卿) The Injustice to Dou E (竇娥冤) is its best-known work.

The Mongol empire joined up the whole of Eurasia, and merchants, missionaries and envoys could travel between east and west on safe roads. The Venetian merchant is said to have lived in Yuan China for seventeen years, and his account of his travels filled Europeans with longing for the rich East. The Yuan also printed paper money in large quantities, and in its later years printed so much that it caused inflation.

1344 – 1368

The fall of the Yuan

In the later Yuan, Mongol nobles fought over the throne, government fell into chaos, and natural disasters struck year after year. In 1344 the Yellow River burst its banks and changed course, flooding huge areas; the court then conscripted well over a hundred thousand labourers to work on the river, and anger boiled over. In 1351 a rebellion broke out, centred on a religious group called the (白蓮教). The rebels wrapped their heads in red cloth and were called the . It is said that before the rising, someone buried a one-eyed stone figure in the bed of the Yellow River, with words carved on its back: "Don't laugh at the stone man for having one eye; stir up the Yellow River and all under Heaven will rise." When the labourers dug it up, they took it as a sign from Heaven.

Among all the rebel armies, one man rose above the rest: (朱元璋), born into a poor farming family, who had once been a Buddhist monk. In 1368 he founded the Ming dynasty (明朝) in Nanjing (南京), and the same year sent an army to take Dadu. The last Yuan emperor retreated to the Mongolian steppe (see Era 7).

Daily life

How people lived

The night markets of Kaifeng

The night markets of Northern Song Kaifeng stayed open until the third watch of the night (around midnight), and the morning markets opened at the fifth watch (before dawn), so trade hardly stopped at all. The streets sold mutton, dumplings, "soup cakes" (湯餅, noodles), fruit and all kinds of snacks; in the teahouses storytellers told tales of the Three Kingdoms (三國), and a single wazi might have dozens of stages going at once. City people began to eat three meals a day, and to eat at tables sitting on high chairs, instead of sitting on mats on the floor as people had before the Tang.

Tea and porcelain

Everyone in the Song loved tea. The fashion was to grind the leaves into a powder and whisk it with boiling water into a froth, known as "whisked tea" (點茶), and scholars even held "tea contests" (鬥茶) to judge whose froth was best. This way of drinking tea later spread to Japan and developed into the Japanese tea ceremony. Song porcelain is famous for its elegant single-colour glazes; later generations called the Ru (汝), Guan (官), Ge (哥), Jun (鈞) and Ding (定) wares the "Five Great Kilns" (五大名窯) of the Song.

Women's lives

The Song produced China's most famous woman poet, (李清照). She and her husband collected bronze and stone inscriptions, books and paintings together; after his death she was driven from place to place by war. In the same period, the custom of (纏足, chanzu), wrapping girls' feet tightly in cloth to make them small and misshapen, gradually became fashionable among the upper classes. Under the Ming and Qing (清) it spread to every class, and it was not abolished until the twentieth century.

Key people

Who's who

  • Emperor Taizu of Song (宋太祖; Zhao Kuangyin 趙匡胤)Founder of the Song, 927–976

    Became emperor through the Chenqiao Mutiny, took back the generals' commands "over a cup of wine", and set the state policy of putting civil officials above the military.

  • Wang Anshi (王安石)Chief minister and reformer, 1021–1086

    Led reforms intended to make the state rich and its army strong, which set off a factional struggle lasting decades. He was also a first-rate poet and essayist.

  • Su Shi (蘇軾, Su Dongpo)Writer and calligrapher, 1037–1101

    The most versatile man of letters of the Song, first-rate in poetry, song lyrics, prose, calligraphy and painting. He was banished many times but always stayed cheerful and broad-minded.

  • Yue Fei (岳飛)General against the Jin, 1103–1142

    Beat the Jin army again and again, was put to death by Qin Hui on a charge of "perhaps there was", and became one of China's most revered national heroes.

  • Zhu Xi (朱熹)Thinker, 1130–1200

    Brought the Learning of Principle to its fullest form. His commentaries on the Four Books became the standard for the civil service examination for the next six or seven hundred years.

  • Genghis Khan (成吉思汗; Temujin 鐵木真)Founder of the Mongol empire, c. 1162–1227

    United the Mongol tribes and built a great empire that conquered much of Eurasia. In his lifetime he did not conquer most of China; the Yuan dynasty was founded by his grandson.

  • Kublai Khan (忽必烈)Emperor Shizu of Yuan, 1215–1294

    Grandson of Genghis Khan. He founded the Yuan dynasty with its capital at Dadu (Beijing), destroyed the Southern Song in 1279 and united China.

Myth or fact

What people often get wrong

  • "The Song was poor and weak."Only half right. The Song was indeed often on the losing side militarily, but its economy, technology and culture were highly advanced, and the government's tax revenue was far greater than the Tang's. "Weak" is true mainly in comparison with the powerful horse-riding peoples of the north; "poor" is simply not true.
  • "Yue Fei's mother tattooed 'Serve the country with utmost loyalty' on his back."The tattoo is real, but his mother did not do it. The histories record that Yue Fei's back did carry four characters meaning "serve the country with utmost loyalty" (盡忠報國), but they do not say who tattooed them. The story of his mother doing it was added later by novels and operas.
  • "Marco Polo brought noodles to Italy."Wrong. There are records of dried pasta in Italy before Marco Polo. Scholars even argue about whether Marco Polo really went to China at all: his book does not mention the Great Wall, tea or foot-binding. But many of its details do match Chinese sources, and most scholars still believe he went.
  • "Genghis Khan was an emperor of China."Not accurate. Genghis Khan was the Great Khan of the Mongol empire, and in his lifetime he conquered only part of northern China. Only after the Yuan dynasty was founded was he given the posthumous title "Emperor Taizu of Yuan" (元太祖). Both Mongolia and China see him as part of their own history, and this is still a sensitive subject.
How we know

The age of print

A flood of books

Once printing spread, the Song left far more histories, local gazetteers, notebooks, collected writings and poems than any earlier period. Sima Guang's Comprehensive Mirror for Aid in Government, Shen Kuo's Dream Pool Essays, and Meng Yuanlao's (孟元老) Dreams of Splendour in the Eastern Capital (東京夢華錄), his memories of the rich life of Northern Song Kaifeng, all let us understand the society of the time in detail.

Paintings

Realistic paintings such as Along the River During the Qingming Festival record the streets, bridges, boats, shops and clothing of Song towns and countryside. Song landscape and bird-and-flower paintings are also high points of Chinese art, and many are kept in the two Palace Museums (故宮博物院), in Beijing and Taipei (台北).

Shipwrecks

In 2007 the Nanhai No. 1 was lifted out of the sea in one piece and placed in a huge water tank inside a museum, where it is being excavated slowly. Its cargo, the passengers' belongings and the structure of the ship show Song sea trade directly. A Yuan shipwreck found off Sinan in South Korea also carried about twenty thousand pieces of Chinese porcelain.

Key terms

Terms from this era

Northern Song / Southern Song (北宋 / 南宋)
The two halves of the Song dynasty, divided by the fall of Kaifeng in 1127, with capitals at Kaifeng and Hangzhou respectively.
Civil over military (重文輕武)
The Song state policy of having civil officials run the state and the army, and keeping generals in check.
Scholar-officials (士大夫)
Educated men who became officials through the civil service examination, and were both officials and scholars.
Jiaozi (交子)
The paper money of Sichuan under the Northern Song, the world's first paper money issued by a government.
Neo-Confucianism (理學)
The new Confucianism developed under the Song, represented above all by Zhu Xi, which shaped East Asia for centuries.
Maritime Trade Office (市舶司)
The government office that managed overseas trade by sea and collected customs duties.
Cathay (契丹)
The medieval European name for northern China, from "Khitan" (契丹).
Yuan drama (元曲)
The plays and songs popular under the Yuan, the first high point of Chinese drama.
Hong Kong

Hong Kong in the Song

By the Song, Hong Kong already had settled communities and industries. The Tang (鄧) clan moved from Jiangxi (江西) to Kam Tin (錦田) in the New Territories (新界) during the Northern Song, and later became the first of the New Territories' "Five Great Clans" (五大氏族); walled villages such as Kat Hing Wai (吉慶圍) in Kam Tin are still home to the Tang clan today. The government set up the Kwun Fu Cheung (官富場) salt field around Kowloon Bay (九龍灣) to produce sea salt, with officials sent to run it. The rock inscription at Joss House Bay (北佛堂石刻) in Sai Kung (西貢) was carved in 1274 and records a visit by a salt official; it is the earliest dated inscription known in Hong Kong.

In 1277–78 the Southern Song court, fleeing the Yuan army with two boy emperors, stopped for a time around Kowloon. Sung Wong Toi (宋王臺, "Terrace of the Song Kings") in Kowloon City (九龍城) was originally a huge seaside boulder carved with those three characters, in memory of this episode. During the Second World War the Japanese army blew up the boulder to extend Kai Tak (啟德) airport; the inscription in today's Sung Wong Toi Garden was cut from the surviving part of the original rock. The "Lingding Sea" in Wen Tianxiang's poem is the Lingding Yang (伶仃洋), the stretch of water just west of Hong Kong.

Meanwhile in Europe and the world

A world joined up by the Mongols

The Norman Conquest and Magna Carta

In 1066, three years before Wang Anshi's reforms began under the Northern Song, the Normans conquered England. From 1096 Europe launched the Crusades. Under the Southern Song, in 1215, England's barons forced King John to seal Magna Carta, limiting the king's power. Scotland kept its independence when Robert the Bruce defeated the English army at the Battle of Bannockburn in 1314; China was then under the Yuan.

Mongol armies reach Europe

In 1241 the Mongols crushed European armies in what are now Poland and Hungary and pushed on towards Vienna, shocking all of Europe. Then they suddenly withdrew, because the Great Khan Ögedei (窩闊台) had died and the Mongol commanders had to go home to choose a new one. In 1258 the Mongols took Baghdad and killed the caliph of the Abbasid dynasty, bringing the golden age of the Islamic world to an end.

The Black Death

From 1347 plague came into Europe from the Black Sea, and within a few years it killed between a third and a half of Europe's population; it reached Scotland in 1349. The plague probably began in Central Asia and spread along the trade routes of the Mongol empire. China also recorded severe epidemics in the same period, but scholars still argue about whether it was the same disease.

Places to visit

  • Quanzhou: Emporium of the World in Song-Yuan China (泉州：宋元中國的世界海洋商貿中心)Quanzhou, Fujian

    A World Heritage Site since 2021, including the Qingjing Mosque (清淨寺), Kaiyuan Temple (開元寺), the old harbour, the site of the customs office and gravestones of foreigners from the Song and Yuan.

  • Maritime Silk Road Museum of Guangdong (廣東海上絲綢之路博物館)Yangjiang, Guangdong

    The Nanhai No. 1 wreck sits in the museum's "Crystal Palace", where you can watch archaeologists excavating it.

  • The Palace Museums (故宮博物院)Beijing; Taipei

    The Palace Museum in Beijing holds Along the River During the Qingming Festival; the National Palace Museum in Taipei holds a great deal of Song painting and calligraphy and Ru ware. Both put works on display in rotation.

  • Temple of Yue Fei and West Lake (岳王廟與西湖)Hangzhou, Zhejiang

    Yue Fei's tomb and temple, with iron statues of Qin Hui, his wife and others kneeling in front of the tomb. Hangzhou was Lin'an, the Southern Song capital, and the Su Causeway (蘇堤) on West Lake was built by Su Shi when he governed Hangzhou.

  • Sung Wong Toi Garden and Ping Shan Heritage Trail (宋王臺花園及屏山文物徑)Kowloon City; Yuen Long, Hong Kong

    The Sung Wong Toi inscription; the Ping Shan Heritage Trail has Tang clan ancestral halls, a walled village and Tsui Sing Lau (聚星樓), Hong Kong's only ancient pagoda.

  • British Museum, China galleryLondon · free

    The Chinese ceramics collection given by Sir Percival David includes many masterpieces from the famous Song kilns, among them Ru ware.

The one thing to rememberThe Song could not match the horse-riding peoples of the north in war, but it reached a peak in economy, technology and culture; in the end the Mongols conquered all of China, founded the Yuan, and joined China to the whole of Eurasia.

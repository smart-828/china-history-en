Era 8

The Qing Dynasty

1644 – 1912

The Qing (清) was China's last imperial dynasty. Its rulers were not the Han Chinese who made up most of the population, but the (滿族), a much smaller people from the northeast. In its early years the Qing was immensely powerful, and its territory was the largest in Chinese history. But from the nineteenth century, Britain and other industrialised Western countries forced China open with and warships, and rebellion at home came on top of invasion from abroad. The Qing tried to reform several times and failed. In 1912, more than two thousand years of rule by emperors came to an end.

1616 – 1644

Who were the Manchus?

North of the Great Wall (長城), in what is now northeast China, lived a people called the (女真). They lived by hunting, fishing and farming, and were split into many rival tribes. In the late sixteenth century a chief named (努爾哈赤) united the tribes and organised his whole people into military units called the (八旗): each banner was at once an army unit and a unit for living and administration. In 1616 he founded a state called Jin (金), known to historians as the Later Jin (後金).

His son (皇太極) renamed the people "Manchu" (滿洲) in 1635, and the next year renamed the state "Qing". At that time there were only a few hundred thousand Manchus, while the Ming (明) dynasty south of the Great Wall ruled over a hundred million people. By any normal reckoning, the Manchus had no chance of conquering China. But the Ming was falling apart from within.

1644

A gate that changed history

The last years of the Ming brought disaster after disaster, and starving peasants rose in revolt. In the spring of 1644 the rebel leader (李自成) captured Beijing (北京). The last Ming emperor, (崇禎), hanged himself on a small hill behind the palace, and the Ming dynasty was over.

At that moment the Ming's best army was on the northeastern frontier, holding off the Qing. Its commander was General (吳三桂), stationed at (山海關): the fortified pass at the eastern end of the Great Wall, and the only road from the northeast to Beijing. With Beijing fallen, Wu Sangui had to choose: surrender to Li Zicheng, or go over to the Qing army outside the wall? In the end he opened the gate, and together with the Qing he defeated Li Zicheng.

Once inside Beijing, the Qing army never left. In the autumn of 1644 the Qing moved its capital to Beijing, and the six-year-old (順治) Emperor was enthroned, with his uncle (多爾袞) ruling as regent. A people of fewer than a million had begun to rule a country of more than a hundred million.

1645 – 1662

"Keep your hair and lose your head"

Manchu men traditionally shaved the front half of the head and wore the rest of the hair in a long braid. In 1645 the Qing issued the (薙髮令, tifaling), requiring every Han Chinese man to do the same within ten days as a sign of submission, on pain of death. People summed it up as "keep your hair and lose your head, or keep your head and lose your hair". To Han Chinese, hair was a gift from one's parents, and shaving it was a deep humiliation, so many places resisted fiercely. When the Qing army took the city of Yangzhou (揚州), near the Yangtze (長江), it massacred much of the population, an event remembered as the "Ten Days of Yangzhou" (揚州十日).

Members of the Ming royal family set up a series of regimes in the south, known as the (南明), but within about twenty years they had all been destroyed. The most famous resistance leader was (鄭成功), known in the West as Koxinga, the son of a Fujian (福建) merchant-pirate and a Japanese mother. In 1662 he took Taiwan (台灣) from the Dutch and made it his base against the Qing.

1661 – 1689

The young Kangxi Emperor

In 1661 the eight-year-old (康熙) Emperor came to the throne. ("Kangxi" was actually the name of his reign, his (年號, nianhao), not his personal name; Ming and Qing emperors are usually known by their reign titles.) He reigned for sixty-one years, longer than any other Chinese emperor.

Kangxi's first big problem was a legacy of the conquest. When the Qing entered China, several Ming generals had changed sides to help them. As a reward, the Qing let three of them stay in the south as "feudatory princes" (藩王), each with his own army and tax revenue, in effect ruling three semi-independent states: Wu Sangui (the same general who had opened the gate at Shanhaiguan in 1644) in Yunnan (雲南), Shang Kexi (尚可喜) in Guangdong (廣東) and Geng Jingzhong (耿精忠) in Fujian. The young Kangxi saw them as a threat to central power, and in 1673 decided to abolish their states. Wu Sangui and the others immediately rose against the Qing in what became known as the (三藩之亂). The war lasted eight years and was only put down in 1681.

Two years later a Qing fleet captured Taiwan from Zheng Chenggong's descendants. The next year the Qing made Taiwan a prefecture of Fujian province: the first time it had formally become part of a Chinese imperial state. In the north, Russians pushing east across Siberia were clashing with Qing troops along the Amur River (黑龍江). In 1689 the two empires fixed their border in the (尼布楚條約). Neither side spoke the other's language, so the negotiations were conducted in Latin, through European Jesuit missionaries who worked at the Qing court.

1722 – 1796

The golden age: Yongzheng and Qianlong

Kangxi's successor, the (雍正) Emperor, reigned for thirteen years and was famous for his hard work, personally reading and answering stacks of "memorials" (reports sent to the emperor by officials) every day. He set up the (軍機處, junjichu), a small group of trusted ministers who met the emperor every day, making decisions faster and more secret. He also brought in a (攤丁入畝, tanding rumu) that abolished the poll tax on every person and folded it into the land tax, greatly easing the burden on the landless poor.

Yongzheng's son, the (乾隆) Emperor, reigned for sixty years. He destroyed the (準噶爾), a powerful Mongol state in the northwest, and brought the lands north and south of the Tianshan (天山) mountains into the empire under the name (新疆), meaning "new frontier". The Qing empire now covered about thirteen million square kilometres, the largest in Chinese history. So as not to reign longer than his grandfather Kangxi, Qianlong handed the throne to his son after sixty years, but in practice he held on to power until his death in 1799.

More than a century of peace, together with sweet potatoes and maize (which had arrived from the Americas under the Ming and could grow on hillsides), made the population soar: from about 150 million around 1700 to about 300 million by the end of the eighteenth century. Qianlong ordered a vast collection of books, the (四庫全書), copying out more than three thousand titles. At the same time he carried out (文字獄, wenziyu): anyone whose writing seemed disrespectful to the Manchus or the Qing could be put to death, and thousands of books were banned or destroyed.

1757 – 1793

Trade only at Canton

In the eighteenth century the British drank more and more Chinese tea, and bought large amounts of silk and porcelain. From 1757 Qianlong ruled that all Western merchant ships could trade at only one port, Guangzhou (廣州), which Westerners called Canton, under what was known as the (一口通商, yikou tongshang). Foreign merchants could only deal with a small number of government-licensed Chinese merchants (the merchants, 十三行). They had to live in a narrow strip of warehouses and offices on the riverfront outside the city walls, were not allowed into the city, and could not bring their wives. When the trading season ended, they had to leave and spend the winter in Macau (澳門), which was run by the Portuguese.

The British government resented these restrictions. In 1793 King George III sent Lord (馬戛爾尼) to China with an embassy bearing gifts, including telescopes, a planetarium and clocks. He asked for more ports to be opened, a permanent embassy in Beijing and lower duties. There was also a dispute over etiquette: the Qing demanded that envoys perform the (叩頭, koutou) before the emperor, while Macartney would only kneel on one knee, as he would before his own king. Qianlong turned down every request. In his letter to George III he wrote that "the Celestial Empire possesses all things in abundance": China had everything it needed and had no need of trade with Britain.

1820s – 1842

The Opium War

The British had to pay for Chinese tea in silver, because the Chinese were not interested in British goods, so silver kept flowing into China. Britain's solution was opium, an addictive drug made from the poppy. Britain's (東印度公司) grew opium on a huge scale in India, and British merchants smuggled it into China (the biggest firm, Jardine Matheson, was founded by two Scots). By the 1830s thirty to forty thousand chests were arriving every year, the number of addicts soared, and silver now flowed out of China instead.

In 1839 the (道光) Emperor sent (林則徐), an official famous for his honesty, to Canton to stamp out the trade. Lin besieged the foreign warehouses and forced the merchants to hand over more than twenty thousand chests of opium. At (虎門), near Canton, he dissolved the opium in lime and salt water and flushed it out to sea.

The British government sent a fleet, saying it was protecting its merchants and free trade. British forces had steam-powered iron warships and modern artillery, and the Qing's sailing junks and old cannon could not stop them. The British fought their way up the coast to the outskirts of Nanjing (南京). In 1842 the Qing was forced to sign the (南京條約): Hong Kong Island (香港島) was ceded to Britain; five ports, Guangzhou, Xiamen (廈門), Fuzhou (福州), Ningbo (寧波) and Shanghai (上海), were opened for British trade and residence; China paid an indemnity of 21 million silver dollars; and future customs duties had to be agreed with Britain. A follow-up treaty the next year added that British subjects who broke the law in China would be tried by British officials, not under Chinese law. The Chinese call such treaties, signed under the threat of force and favouring only one side, (不平等條約, bupingdeng tiaoyue).

1851 – 1864

The Taiping: a civil war led by "Jesus's younger brother"

In Guangdong, a scholar called (洪秀全) failed the (科舉, keju) four times. He read a Christian tract written by a Chinese convert and, during a serious illness, saw visions. He came to believe that he was God's second son and the younger brother of Jesus, sent to destroy the "demons" on earth: the Manchu rulers. He founded the God Worshipping Society (拜上帝會), and won large numbers of followers among poor farmers, miners and the Hakka (客家) people.

In 1851 he rose up in Guangxi (廣西) and founded the (太平天國). The disciplined Taiping armies swept north and in 1853 captured Nanjing, which they renamed the "Heavenly Capital" (天京). They banned opium, foot-binding and alcohol, and proclaimed equality between men and women and an equal share of land for all. But the leaders in Nanjing soon lived in luxury, and in 1856 turned on each other in a bloody purge.

The Qing's own Banner armies had become corrupt and useless, and the tide was only turned by regional armies raised by Han Chinese officials. (曾國藩), a scholar from Hunan (湖南), recruited men from his home province to form the (湘軍). His protégé (李鴻章) formed the Huai Army (淮軍); and in Shanghai a force armed with Western guns and led by foreign officers, the (常勝軍), joined the fight. In 1864 Nanjing fell; Hong Xiuquan had died of illness shortly before. The war spread across more than ten provinces and killed an estimated 20 to 30 million people, making it the deadliest war anywhere in the world in the nineteenth century. It also put armies in the hands of Han Chinese officials, and from then on the central government grew steadily weaker.

1856 – 1860

The burning of the Summer Palace

Britain and France felt they had not gained enough from the Treaty of Nanjing. In 1856, using the search of a merchant ship by Chinese officials as an excuse, they started the Second Opium War (第二次鴉片戰爭). In 1860 an Anglo-French army reached Beijing. Earlier, the Qing had seized a British and French negotiating party, and about twenty of them died from mistreatment. In retaliation, the British envoy Lord (額爾金) ordered the burning of the (圓明園): a vast imperial park northwest of Beijing, with hundreds of halls and a group of European-style palaces designed by Jesuit missionaries. The fire burned for days, and countless treasures were looted. Lord Elgin was a Scottish nobleman; his father was the Lord Elgin who took the sculptures of the Parthenon from Athens to Britain.

The Qing was forced to sign the Convention of Peking (北京條約), ceding the southern tip of the Kowloon (九龍) peninsula to Britain and opening more ports, including Tianjin (天津). Russia, claiming credit for "mediating", obtained about one million square kilometres of land north of the Amur River and east of the Ussuri River (烏蘇里江), including what became Vladivostok (海參崴).

1861 – 1895

The Empress Dowager Cixi and "self-strengthening"

In 1861 the (咸豐) Emperor died in exile from the capital, leaving a son aged just five. The boy's mother was one of Xianfeng's consorts, (慈禧). Together with the late emperor's brother, (恭親王), she staged a coup against the appointed regents, and began to rule as Empress Dowager, (垂簾聽政, chuilian tingzheng). Apart from a few years, she remained the real ruler of the Qing until her death in 1908, almost half a century.

After two defeats, Prince Gong, Li Hongzhang and others understood that China had to learn Western technology. For more than thirty years they pursued the (洋務運動). They built arsenals and shipyards in Shanghai, Fuzhou and elsewhere, opened mines, railways and telegraph lines, founded foreign-language schools, and from 1872 sent 120 boys to study in the United States. The (北洋艦隊), built by Li Hongzhang with modern ironclads bought from Britain and Germany, was for a time considered one of the strongest navies in Asia. But the movement borrowed only technology, without changing the political or educational system, and conservatives at court who opposed learning from the West remained powerful.

1894 – 1898

Defeat by Japan

Japan had also been forced open by the West, but from 1868 its Meiji Restoration reformed not just technology but government, schools and the army. In 1894 China and Japan went to war over control of Korea: the (甲午戰爭). The Beiyang Fleet was beaten at sea, and in 1895 it was destroyed at its base at Weihaiwei (威海衛) in Shandong (山東).

Li Hongzhang was sent to Japan to negotiate the (馬關條約): Taiwan and the Penghu (澎湖) Islands were ceded to Japan, China accepted that Korea was no longer its dependency, and China paid an indemnity of 200 million taels of silver, equal to two or three years of Qing government revenue. Being beaten by a neighbour China had long regarded as its pupil shocked the whole country.

Next, the Western powers began marking out " (勢力範圍, shili fanwei)" in China. In 1897–98 Germany seized Jiaozhou Bay (膠州灣; Qingdao, 青島) in Shandong, Russia leased Port Arthur (旅順) and Dalian (大連) in the northeast, France leased Guangzhouwan (廣州灣) in Guangdong, and Britain leased Weihaiwei and Hong Kong's New Territories (新界). Chinese called this being "carved up like a melon", and feared that China would be divided like Africa.

1898 – 1901

A hundred days of reform, and the Boxers

In 1898 Cixi's nephew, the (光緒) Emperor, who was emperor in name, backed (康有為) and his student (梁啟超) in a sweeping reform programme: modern schools, a reformed examination system and support for industry and trade. The reforms threatened too many interests. After just 103 days, Cixi staged a coup and put Guangxu under house arrest; six reformers were executed, and Kang and Liang fled to Japan. This was the (戊戌變法). Guangxu spent ten years under arrest and died in 1908, one day before Cixi. Tests on his remains in 2008 found a lethal dose of arsenic.

Meanwhile, the (義和團, Yihetuan) were rising in the villages of north China: a movement combining martial arts and folk religion, whose members believed their rituals made them immune to swords and bullets. They hated foreign missionaries and Chinese Christians, and under the slogan "Support the Qing, destroy the foreigners" they killed large numbers of both. In 1900 the Boxers entered Beijing and besieged the foreign legations for fifty-five days, and Cixi declared war on the foreign powers. An (八國聯軍) of Britain, France, Germany, Russia, the United States, Japan, Italy and Austria-Hungary captured Beijing, and Cixi fled to Xi'an (西安) with Guangxu. The (辛丑條約) of 1901 imposed an indemnity of 450 million taels of silver, roughly one tael for every person in China.

1901 – 1912

The last reforms and the 1911 Revolution

After this disaster even Cixi agreed to reform, in what was called the (新政, xinzheng): a modern army, students sent abroad and, in 1905, the abolition of the civil service examination after thirteen hundred years, along with a promise of constitutional government. But the "cabinet" formed in 1911 was mostly Manchu princes, and many who had supported reform gave up on the Qing altogether.

Others had long since decided that the Qing had to go. (孫中山), from Guangdong, had studied medicine in Hong Kong and became a professional revolutionary, raising money among overseas Chinese. In 1905 he founded the (同盟會; Revolutionary Alliance) in Tokyo. They launched about ten uprisings, and every one failed.

On 9 October 1911 a bomb being made by revolutionaries in Hankou (漢口) exploded by accident, exposing their plans. On the evening of 10 October, soldiers of the modern army in nearby Wuchang (武昌) were forced to rise early, and to everyone's surprise they succeeded: the (武昌起義). Within two months more than a dozen provinces had declared independence from the Qing. On 1 January 1912 the Republic of China (中華民國) was founded in Nanjing, with Sun Yat-sen as provisional president. (袁世凱), the most powerful Qing official, negotiated with the revolutionaries and persuaded the imperial family to give up the throne. On 12 February 1912 the six-year-old emperor (溥儀) abdicated, and more than two thousand years of imperial rule in China came to an end. Puyi's life later became the subject of the film The Last Emperor.

Daily life

How people lived

Bannermen in Beijing

Manchus and other members of the Eight Banners lived with their families in Beijing's Inner City, supported by state grain and silver stipends and forbidden to trade or take other work; Han Chinese lived in the Outer City. In the late Qing, as their numbers grew and stipends shrank, many bannermen fell on hard times, but they also created a refined culture of leisure, from Peking opera (京劇) to keeping songbirds and cricket fighting.

The foreign factories at Canton

Under the Canton System, foreign merchants spent most of the year in the "factories" (warehouses and offices) on the Canton waterfront, bargaining with the Hong merchants and inspecting goods. One Hong merchant, Howqua (伍秉鑑), became so rich that an American newspaper later listed him among the wealthiest people in history. Foreigners could only stroll in the small square in front of the factories, or visit gardens across the river on approved days.

Seeking a living overseas

From the mid-nineteenth century, large numbers of poor farmers from Guangdong and Fujian went abroad to Southeast Asia, the United States, Canada and Australia, working in gold mines, on plantations and building railways. Many borrowed money for their passage, or were tricked into harsh labour contracts. Many also settled abroad, creating the Chinese communities found around the world today, including Britain's Chinatowns.

Key people

Who's who

  • Kangxi Emperor (康熙)Emperor, 1654–1722

    Took the throne at eight and reigned for sixty-one years. He crushed the Three Feudatories, took Taiwan and studied mathematics and astronomy with European Jesuits. The most admired Qing emperor.

  • Qianlong Emperor (乾隆)Emperor, 1711–1799

    Kangxi's grandson, who reigned for sixty years and took the Qing to its height. In old age he trusted a corrupt favourite, Heshen, and he turned down Britain's requests when he received Macartney.

  • Lin Zexu (林則徐)Official, 1785–1850

    Famous for his honesty and ability. His campaign against opium in Canton sparked the Opium War, after which he was dismissed and exiled. He had foreign books and newspapers translated and is called the first Chinese to "open his eyes to the world".

  • Hong Xiuquan (洪秀全)Taiping Heavenly King, 1814–1864

    A Guangdong scholar who failed the civil service examination, declared himself Jesus's younger brother and founded the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom, holding Nanjing for eleven years.

  • Empress Dowager Cixi (慈禧)Real ruler, 1835–1908

    A consort of the Xianfeng Emperor who held power as Empress Dowager for nearly fifty years. She backed self-strengthening but crushed the Hundred Days' Reform and supported the Boxers. Historians still argue about her.

  • Li Hongzhang (李鴻章)Statesman, 1823–1901

    The most powerful Han Chinese official of the late Qing. He led the Self-Strengthening Movement and built the Beiyang Fleet, but also signed the Treaty of Shimonoseki and the Boxer Protocol. Westerners called him the "Bismarck of the East".

Myth or fact

What people often get wrong

  • "Lin Zexu burned the opium at Humen."Not quite. Burnt opium leaves a residue that can still be smoked. Lin had pits dug and filled with seawater, dissolved the opium with salt and lime, and let the tide carry it out to sea. The Chinese term means "destroying" the opium, not "burning" it.
  • "All of Hong Kong was leased for 99 years."No. Hong Kong Island (1842) and the southern tip of Kowloon (1860) were ceded permanently. Only the New Territories were leased, for 99 years from 1898. But the New Territories make up about 90 per cent of Hong Kong, so when the lease approached its end, the future of all of Hong Kong was in question.
  • "Qing China was completely closed to the outside world."Too simple. The Canton System limited where trade could happen, but exports of tea, silk and porcelain were enormous. Until opium arrived in large quantities, China ran a trade surplus.
  • "Cixi spent the navy's money on the Summer Palace, so China lost to Japan."Partly true. Naval funds were diverted to rebuild the Summer Palace, but historians disagree about how much and how much it mattered. The deeper causes of defeat were command, training, corruption and the whole system.
  • "The long braid was a traditional Chinese hairstyle."No. The braid, or queue, was a Manchu style, forced on Han Chinese men by the Qing. Before the Qing, Han men wore their long hair tied up on top of the head. That is why cutting off the queue became a symbol of support for the republic after 1911.
How we know

Archives, photographs and newspapers

The emperor's comments

The First Historical Archives of China (中國第一歷史檔案館) in Beijing holds about ten million Ming and Qing documents, including officials' memorials to the emperor. Emperors wrote their responses on them in red ink, often very bluntly (Yongzheng sometimes scolded his officials in the margins), giving us their real thoughts.

Both sides of the story

The British Foreign Office, the East India Company, missionaries and merchants left huge numbers of documents and letters describing the Opium Wars and treaty negotiations from the other side. Putting Chinese and British records side by side often reveals completely different accounts of the same events.

The first photographs and newspapers

In 1860 the photographer Felice Beato, travelling with the British army, took pictures of Beijing and the battlefields, some of the earliest photographs of China. Chinese-language newspapers such as the Shenbao (申報), founded in Shanghai in 1872, recorded the news and public opinion of the late Qing.

Key terms

Terms from this era

Reign title (年號)
The name an emperor gave to the years of his reign. Ming and Qing emperors used just one each, so they are known by it: Kangxi, Qianlong.
Eight Banners (八旗)
The Qing's military and social organisation. Every Manchu belonged to a banner; there were later Mongol and Han banners too.
Memorial (奏摺)
A written report from an official sent directly to the emperor, who read and annotated it himself.
Unequal treaties (不平等條約)
Treaties forced on the Qing by foreign powers, taking territory, money and rights.
Self-Strengthening (洋務運動)
The reforms of the 1860s to 1890s that adopted Western military and industrial technology.
Concession (租界)
An area in a Chinese treaty port set aside for foreigners and run by them. The largest was in Shanghai.
Hong Kong

Hong Kong becomes a British colony

In January 1841, while the Opium War was still going on, British forces landed on the north shore of Hong Kong Island and raised the flag at a spot still known as Possession Point (水坑口). The island then had about seven thousand inhabitants, mostly fishermen, stonecutters and farmers. The Treaty of Nanjing ceded Hong Kong Island to Britain in 1842, and Britain developed it as a free port without customs duties, drawing in large numbers of Chinese and foreign merchants. With the southern tip of Kowloon ceded in 1860 and the New Territories leased for 99 years in 1898, Hong Kong took roughly its present shape.

In 1870 Chinese merchants founded the Tung Wah Hospital (東華醫院), which offered Chinese medicine and charity and became the leading institution of Hong Kong's Chinese community. In 1894 plague broke out in Hong Kong, and the Swiss-French bacteriologist Alexandre Yersin identified the plague bacterium there for the first time. Sun Yat-sen went to secondary school and medical college in Hong Kong, and later said that his revolutionary ideas were born there.

Meanwhile in Europe and the world

An industrial West and a reforming Asia

An age of revolutions

During Qianlong's reign, Britain began its Industrial Revolution, powered by the steam engine improved by the Scot James Watt; the United States declared independence in 1776, and the French Revolution broke out in 1789. When Macartney arrived in China, Europe was being transformed by new technology and new political ideas, and the Qing knew almost nothing about it.

Japan's Meiji Restoration

In 1853 an American fleet sailed into Tokyo Bay and forced Japan to open up. From 1868 the Meiji Restoration reformed not just technology but government, education and the army, and sent large numbers of students abroad. The different results of the two countries' reforms became clear in the war of 1894.

Carving up the world

In 1884–85 the European powers met in Berlin and divided Africa into colonies. A little over ten years later they marked out spheres of influence in China, and Chinese intellectuals feared that China would be the next Africa.

Places to visit

  • Old Summer Palace (圓明園)Beijing

    The imperial park burned in 1860. The broken columns of the European-style palaces still stand.

  • The Palace Museum (故宮博物院; Forbidden City, 紫禁城)Beijing

    The palace of the Ming and Qing emperors. Puyi went on living here after his abdication, until 1924.

  • Chengde Mountain Resort (承德避暑山莊)Hebei

    A World Heritage Site: the summer palace of Kangxi and Qianlong, where Macartney met Qianlong.

  • Opium War Museum (鴉片戰爭博物館)Humen, Guangdong

    The site of Lin Zexu's pools for destroying opium, and the Humen forts.

  • Hong Kong Museum of Coastal Defence (香港海防博物館)Shau Kei Wan, Hong Kong

    Built in a late nineteenth-century fort at Lei Yue Mun (鯉魚門), covering six hundred years of Hong Kong's coastal defence.

  • Dr Sun Yat-sen Museum (孫中山紀念館)Mid-Levels, Hong Kong

    Tells the story of Sun Yat-sen and Hong Kong, with a heritage trail nearby.

The one thing to rememberA small Manchu people built the largest empire in Chinese history. In the nineteenth century, under pressure from industrialised foreign powers, the Qing's reforms failed again and again; imperial rule ended in 1912, and Hong Kong became a British colony along the way.

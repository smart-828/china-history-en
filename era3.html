Era 3

Spring and Autumn, Warring States

770 – 221 BC

After the Zhou (周) royal house moved east, the Zhou king kept only a title. The regional lords swallowed one another up, and for more than five hundred years war hardly stopped: more than a hundred states shrank to seven, and in the end to one. Yet this was also the liveliest age of ideas in Chinese history. Confucius (孔子), Laozi (老子), Mozi (墨子), Mencius (孟子), Han Fei (韓非) and others all put forward their own answers on how to govern and how to live, an age known as the "Hundred Schools of Thought" (百家爭鳴, baijia zhengming), at roughly the same time as Socrates and Plato in ancient Greece. In the end the western state of Qin (秦), made the strongest by sweeping reforms, unified the country in 221 BC.

Before we start

Two names for one long age of turmoil

In 770 BC the Zhou royal house was forced to move its capital from Haojing (鎬京) in the west to Luoyi (洛邑) in the east (see Era 2), and from then on the dynasty is called the Eastern Zhou (東周). The Eastern Zhou is itself split into two halves. The first half is called the (春秋), after a chronicle from the state of Lu (魯) called the Spring and Autumn Annals (春秋, Chunqiu). The second half is called the (戰國), after a book compiled in the Han dynasty (漢), the Strategies of the Warring States (戰國策, Zhanguo ce): the name means "states at war with one another". The dividing line is usually put at around 475 BC.

Throughout this time the Zhou king was still in name the "Son of Heaven" (天子, tianzi), and every state recognised his position. But he controlled only a small patch of land around Luoyang (洛陽), with no army of his own and no power over the lords. It was a little like the emperor of the in medieval Europe: a grand title, while the real power lay with princes and lords across the land.

At the start of the Spring and Autumn period China had more than a hundred states, large and small. After centuries of swallowing one another up, by the Warring States period only seven large states and a few small ones were left, and in the end all of them were unified by Qin (see Map 1).

770 – 476 BC

Spring and Autumn: the age of the hegemons

The Zhou king was too weak to keep order, so powerful lords stepped forward as leaders, called (霸主, bazhu). A hegemon summoned the rulers of the other states to meetings to make alliances, settled disputes and led them against outside enemies, under the slogan (尊王攘夷, zunwang rangyi): respect the Zhou king and fight off foreign peoples. The first hegemon was (齊桓公), ruler of the eastern state of Qi (齊). He made (管仲), a man of common birth, his chief minister, and Guan Zhong built up fishing and salt production and made Qi rich and strong. Confucius later said that without Guan Zhong, we would all be living under foreign rule.

After that, the northern state of Jin (晉) became the strongest. (晉文公) had been driven abroad as a young man by court power struggles and spent nineteen years in exile, coming home to take the throne only in his sixties, yet within a few years he became hegemon. In the south, in the Yangtze (長江, Changjiang) valley, the state of (楚) also rose fast. The northern states regarded Chu as half "barbarian", but it was large, rich in resources and had a culture all its own. Its ruler, (楚莊王), once led his army to the outskirts of Luoyang and asked the Zhou king's envoy how large and how heavy the Zhou royal house's nine sacred bronze cauldrons (九鼎, jiuding) were, a hint that he wanted to replace the Zhou king. The Chinese expression "asking about the cauldrons" (問鼎), meaning to aim for supreme power, still comes from this story.

At the end of the Spring and Autumn period the southeastern states of Wu (吳) and Yue (越) (in today's Jiangsu (江蘇) and Zhejiang (浙江)) fought a famous war of revenge. (勾踐), King of Yue, was defeated by the King of Wu and forced to surrender. It is said that after he returned home he tasted a piece of bitter gall every day to remind himself never to forget the humiliation, and twenty years later he finally destroyed Wu. The Chinese saying "sleeping on brushwood and tasting gall" (臥薪嘗膽) means enduring hardship and humiliation while working to rise again. The (越王勾踐劍), dug up in Hubei (湖北) in 1965, had lain buried for more than two thousand years, yet when it was found it had no rust and was still extremely sharp.

551 – 479 BC

Confucius: a teacher who failed in his lifetime

(孔子, Kongzi) is the most influential thinker in Chinese history. His English name comes from the Catholic missionaries who came to China in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and Latinised "Kong Fuzi" (孔夫子, Master Kong). He was born in the state of Lu (today's Qufu (曲阜) in Shandong (山東)). His father was a low-ranking warrior who died when Confucius was three, and the family was poor. As a young man he held minor posts looking after granaries and pastures, but he was a passionate learner.

In Confucius's time, lords did not respect the Zhou king, ministers seized power from their rulers and the old order was crumbling: people at the time described it as "ritual collapsing and music in ruins" (禮崩樂壞). Confucius believed the answer was to return to the (禮, li) laid down by the Duke of Zhou (周公), with everyone playing their proper part: a ruler should act as a ruler, a minister as a minister, a father as a father and a son as a son. The virtue he valued most was (仁), caring for others. He said, "Do not do to others what you would not want done to yourself": what you do not want yourself, do not force on others. This is very close to what the West calls the "Golden Rule". His ideal person was the (君子): a person of good character and cultivation, not merely a person of noble birth.

All his life Confucius wanted an official post so he could put his ideas into practice, but he had little success. In his fifties he left Lu with his students and travelled from state to state for fourteen years, hoping to find a ruler who would employ him. He was turned away everywhere, and once he was even surrounded and cut off from food. In old age he returned to Lu and devoted himself to teaching and to editing ancient texts. He held that : anyone willing to learn could be his student, whatever their background, breaking the nobility's monopoly on education. His sayings and deeds were collected by his students and their students into the (論語, Lunyu). Only more than three hundred years after his death did his teachings become the state's official ideology (see Era 4), and for the next two thousand years they shaped China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam.

c. 500 – 300 BC

Laozi and Zhuangzi: going with nature

In contrast to Confucius, (老子) believed society was in chaos precisely because people chased too many rules, too much knowledge and too many desires. He is said to have been an official in charge of the Zhou royal library, and he left a book of about five thousand characters, the (道德經). He said that everything in the universe follows an invisible pattern, called the (道, "the Way"), and that the best ruler practises (無為), not interfering on purpose and letting the people live naturally. However, almost nothing about Laozi's life can be verified, and whether the book was written by one person at one time is also disputed.

(莊子), who lived in the Warring States period, carried on and developed Laozi's ideas. He made his points through a great many parables, the most famous being "Zhuangzi dreams of a butterfly" (莊周夢蝶): Zhuangzi dreamed he had become a butterfly, and when he woke up he did not know whether he was Zhuangzi who had dreamed of being a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming of being Zhuangzi. This school of thought later became known as (道家), also spelled Taoism in English. It stresses going with nature and not caring much for fame and fortune, and it deeply influenced Chinese poetry, painting and traditional medicine. It is also one of the sources of the later Daoist religion.

475 – 221 BC

The seven Warring States: wars grow ever larger

Towards the end of the Spring and Autumn period, great noble families inside the strong states gradually took power away from their rulers. Jin, the strongest state in the north, was carved up by three great families, Han (韓), Zhao (趙) and Wei (魏), an event known as the ; in the east, the ruler of Qi was replaced by the Tian (田) family of ministers. By the Warring States period there were seven great states: (秦) in the west, Chu in the south, Qi in the east, Yan (燕) in the northeast, and Han, Zhao and Wei in the middle, together called the (戰國七雄, zhanguo qixiong) (see Map 1).

The way wars were fought changed completely too. In the Spring and Autumn period battles were mainly fought by nobles in horse-drawn chariots, with a strong sense of etiquette, and a war was often over in a single day. In the Warring States period tools and weapons became common, and states organised huge numbers of peasants into infantry armed with long halberds and the , a bow fired by a trigger mechanism. Chinese armies used crossbows on a large scale more than a thousand years before Europe. A single campaign could involve more than a hundred thousand men, and a war could go on for years. (趙武靈王), in the north, saw how nimbly the horsemen of the northern grasslands fought, and ordered his soldiers to wear the grassland peoples' short tunics and trousers and learn to shoot from horseback, a reform known as "nomad dress and mounted archery" (胡服騎射, hufu qishe). The states also built long walls along their borders, the forerunners of the later Great Wall (長城).

356 – 338 BC

Shang Yang's reforms: why did Qin become the strongest?

Under fierce competition, every state tried to carry out (變法, bianfa), an overhaul of the whole system, to make the country rich and its army strong. The western state of Qin went furthest. Qin had been a backward state on the fringes, looked down on by the states of the east. In 361 BC a foreigner named (商鞅) arrived in Qin, won the trust of its young ruler, (秦孝公), and was put in charge of reform.

To make people believe the new laws meant exactly what they said, Shang Yang is said to have set up a wooden pole at the south gate of the capital and announced that whoever carried it to the north gate would be rewarded with ten pieces of gold. Nobody believed him, so he raised the reward to fifty. At last someone carried the pole across, and Shang Yang paid him the full amount on the spot. This is the story of "moving the pole to win trust" (移木立信).

The main points of Shang Yang's reforms were:

  • Rank for military merit: nobles' status was no longer inherited; anyone who killed enemies on the battlefield could rise in rank and receive land.
  • Encouraging farming: those who worked hard on the land and delivered more grain were excused labour service; merchants and idlers were punished.
  • Setting up (縣, xian): the whole state was divided into more than thirty counties, run by officials appointed directly by the ruler rather than granted to nobles.
  • (連坐, lianzuo): people were organised into groups of five or ten households to keep watch on one another; if one person broke the law and the others in the group did not report it, they were punished too.
  • Standard weights and measures: the whole state used the same standard units of length, volume and weight.

From then on Qin became an efficient war machine, but its laws were also extremely harsh. After Duke Xiao died, nobles whom Shang Yang had offended falsely accused him of treason, and he was executed by being torn apart by chariots. But his new laws remained in force, and more than a hundred years later they helped Qin unify the country.

c. 500 – 230 BC

The Hundred Schools of Thought: how can the world be at peace?

Every ruler needed talented people, so learned men could travel from state to state, selling their ideas to rulers and staying on as officials wherever they were appreciated. The eastern state of Qi even set up the (稷下學宮) in its capital, Linzi (臨淄), supporting scholars from everywhere and letting them debate freely, a little like the academies of ancient Athens. Later generations called this free competition of ideas the . The four most influential schools were these (see Figure 3):

Confucianism (儒家, rujia): the followers of Confucius. In the Warring States period (孟子, Mengzi) argued that human nature is good, and that rulers should govern with benevolence. He said, "The people matter most, the state comes next, and the ruler matters least": the people are more important than the state or the ruler, and if a tyrant loses the people's hearts, it is right for them to overthrow him. Another Confucian, (荀子), argued instead that human nature is bad and must be reshaped through ritual and education.

Mohism (墨家, mojia): its founder, (墨子), came from a craftsman background. He taught (兼愛, jian'ai), loving all people equally whether close to you or not, and "non-aggression" (非攻, feigong), opposing wars of conquest. His followers were highly disciplined and often helped weaker states defend their cities. Mohist writings also record knowledge of optics and mechanics, for example that light passing through a small hole forms an upside-down image.

Legalism (法家, fajia): it held that a state should be governed by strict laws, rewards and punishments, and that the ruler's power should be strengthened. Shang Yang was its leading example. (韓非), a student of Xunzi at the end of the Warring States period, brought Legalist thought together into a complete system, and Ying Zheng (嬴政), the King of Qin, greatly admired his writings when he read them.

Daoism: the ideas of Laozi and Zhuangzi (see above). There was also the military thinker (孫子, Sunzi), whose (孫子兵法) stresses that "know the enemy and know yourself, and in a hundred battles you will never be in danger". It is still studied at military academies and business schools around the world.

260 BC

The Battle of Changping: the bloodiest battle

In the later Warring States period, Qin kept expanding eastwards. The six eastern states tried at times to join forces against Qin, a strategy called the (合縱, hezong); Qin, for its part, tried to make separate alliances with individual states and pick them off one by one, a strategy called the "horizontal alliance" (連橫, lianheng). But the six states distrusted one another, and their alliances always fell apart quickly.

In 260 BC Qin and the northern state of Zhao fought a decisive battle at Changping (長平, in today's Gaoping (高平), Shanxi (山西)). Zhao's position had been firmly held by the veteran general Lian Po (廉頗), but Qin spread rumours that led the King of Zhao to replace him with the young (趙括). Zhao Kuo had read every book on warfare but had no experience of real battle, and he was surrounded by the famous Qin general (白起). According to the Records of the Grand Historian (史記, Shiji), after the Zhao army surrendered, four hundred thousand men were buried alive by the Qin army. The figure may be exaggerated, but pits of human bones found there in 1995 confirm that a mass killing really did take place. After this battle, no single state could stand up to Qin on its own. The Chinese saying "talking about war on paper" (紙上談兵) uses Zhao Kuo as the example of someone who knows only theory and cannot put it into practice.

433 – 278 BC

The culture of Chu in the south: bells and a poet

The culture of Chu, on the middle Yangtze, was very different from the north: full of myth, and fond of brightly coloured lacquerware and silk. In 1978 archaeologists in Suizhou (隨州), Hubei, found the tomb of (曾侯乙), ruler of a small state near Chu, buried in 433 BC. It contained a complete set of (編鐘, bianzhong): sixty-five bronze bells hung on a three-tier wooden frame, weighing more than 2,500 kilograms in all. Each bell gives two different notes when struck in two different places, the whole set spans five octaves, and it can still be played today.

The most famous figure of Chu is the poet (屈原). A Chu nobleman, he argued for an alliance with Qi against Qin, but the king pushed him aside and sent him into exile. In exile he wrote the long poem (離騷, Li Sao), expressing his fears for his country and his lonely anger. In 278 BC Qin captured the Chu capital, Ying (郢), and Qu Yuan, it is said, drowned himself in grief in the Miluo River (汨羅江). Legend says the local people rowed out in boats to save him and threw rice into the river so the fish would not eat his body. This is said to be the origin of dragon boat racing and eating rice dumplings at the (端午節, Duanwu) (see "Hong Kong" below).

256 – 246 BC

Two great waterworks

Qin was not only good at war; it also took farming seriously. In about 256 BC (李冰), the official Qin had appointed in Sichuan (四川), built the (都江堰) irrigation system on the Min River (岷江). Instead of damming the river, it uses a levee shaped like a fish's mouth to split the river in two: one channel carries away floodwater, the other carries water for irrigation. From then on the Chengdu Plain (成都平原) rarely suffered floods or droughts and became known as the "Land of Abundance" (天府之國, tianfu zhi guo). Dujiangyan is still in use today and is a World Heritage Site.

The other project, the (鄭國渠), comes with an interesting story. In about 246 BC the eastern state of Han, afraid of a Qin attack, sent a water engineer named Zheng Guo (鄭國) to Qin to persuade it to dig a long canal, hoping the vast project would drain Qin's manpower and resources. The plot was later discovered, and Zheng Guo said: "This canal may have bought Han a few years, but it will bring Qin benefits for ten thousand generations." Qin let him finish the work, the farmland of the Guanzhong Plain (關中) was irrigated, and Qin grew richer and stronger than ever.

246 – 221 BC

Qin unifies China

In 246 BC the thirteen-year-old (嬴政) became King of Qin. He had been born in Handan (邯鄲), the capital of Zhao, where his father, a Qin prince, was then being held as a hostage. When he came of age, Ying Zheng took power himself, relied on the Legalist (李斯), and from 230 BC launched his wars of unification.

The eastern states were helpless. In 227 BC the crown prince of Yan sent the assassin (荊軻) to get close to the King of Qin on the pretext of presenting a map, with a dagger hidden inside the rolled-up map, an episode remembered as "when the map is unrolled, the dagger appears". The assassination failed, Jing Ke was killed, and the attempt only hastened Yan's destruction. Over ten years Qin destroyed the six states one after another: Han, Zhao, Wei, Chu, Yan and Qi (see Map 1). In 221 BC Qi, the last state, surrendered. More than five hundred years of division were over, and for the first time China became a single unified state. The next era begins with this first "emperor".

Daily life

How people lived

Iron ploughs and oxen

From the late Spring and Autumn period, iron ploughs and hoes gradually became common, and people began using oxen to pull ploughs. Large areas of wasteland were brought under cultivation, and grain harvests rose sharply. Farmers worked the land in small families of about five people, paying grain to the state and serving it with labour and military service. In Qin, an ordinary farmer's son who distinguished himself on the battlefield had a chance to win rank and land.

Busy big cities

Linzi, the capital of Qi, is said to have had seventy thousand households, with so many carts in the streets that their axles knocked together and passers-by rubbed shoulders. Its people played the yu (竽, a mouth organ) and the se (瑟, a zither), held cockfights and dog races, played board games and enjoyed a ball-kicking game called cuju (蹴鞠), which FIFA recognises as one of the earliest origins of football. Each state cast its own metal coins, some shaped like small knives (knife money, 刀幣), others like farm tools (spade money, 布幣).

Travelling scholars and retainers

Educated men no longer served just one state: they could travel from state to state with their ideas, seeking an audience with rulers. Powerful nobles competed to attract talented people and support them in their households as "retainers". Lord Mengchang (孟嘗君) of Qi is said to have had three thousand retainers, among them scholars and swordsmen, but also men whose only skills were imitating a cock's crow or stealing. Yet even these men later saved him in a crisis, which gave rise to the saying "crowing like a cock and stealing like a dog" (雞鳴狗盜).

Key people

Who's who

  • Confucius (孔子)Thinker and teacher, 551–479 BC

    Founder of Confucianism, who taught benevolence (ren) and the rites. Unsuccessful in his lifetime, after his death he was honoured as the "Supreme Sage and Foremost Teacher" (至聖先師) and shaped East Asia for more than two thousand years. Known in Chinese as Kongzi.

  • Laozi (老子)Thinker, dates unknown

    Said to have been an official in charge of the Zhou royal library, the author of the Daodejing and the founder of Daoism. His life and the date of his book are still disputed.

  • Mozi (墨子)Thinker, c. 470–391 BC

    From a craftsman background, he taught universal love and non-aggression, led his followers in helping weak states defend their cities, and also studied optics and mechanics.

  • Sun Wu (孫武, Sun Tzu)Military thinker, c. 500 BC

    Traditionally said to have been a general of the state of Wu and the author of The Art of War. Known in English as Sun Tzu.

  • Shang Yang (商鞅)Reformer, c. 390–338 BC

    Led the reforms in Qin that laid the foundations for Qin's unification of China, but was himself executed after his patron, Duke Xiao of Qin, died.

  • Mencius (孟子)Thinker, c. 372–289 BC

    The most important Confucian after Confucius. He taught that human nature is good and that the people matter most, and held that the people have the right to overthrow a tyrant. Known in Chinese as Mengzi.

  • Qu Yuan (屈原)Poet, c. 340–278 BC

    A Chu nobleman who wrote Encountering Sorrow after being sent into exile. He is China's first great poet known by name, and the Dragon Boat Festival commemorates him.

  • Ying Zheng (嬴政)King of Qin, came to the throne 246 BC

    Came to the throne at thirteen, destroyed the six other states in ten years and unified China in 221 BC, becoming the first emperor, Qin Shi Huang (秦始皇) (see Era 4).

Myth or fact

What people often get wrong

  • "Confucius was greatly honoured in his own lifetime."No. For most of his life Confucius failed to get anywhere. On his travels he was once surrounded and cut off from food, and someone even described him as looking "like a stray dog". It was only in the Han dynasty, more than three hundred years after his death, that the state raised him to supreme status.
  • "Confucius taught blind obedience to rulers and fathers."Too simple. Confucius expected rulers and fathers to do their duty as well; Mencius went further and said a tyrant did not deserve to be called a ruler. The idea of absolute obedience in the "Three Bonds" (三綱, sangang) was developed by Confucian scholars from the Han dynasty onwards.
  • "The Daodejing was written by Laozi alone, at one time."Not settled. A Warring States bamboo-slip copy of the Laozi found at Guodian (郭店), Hubei, in 1993 contains less than today's version, in a different order, which suggests the book may have been added to and edited over a long period.
  • "The Art of War was really written by Sun Bin."Cleared up. Sun Bin (孫臏) was a descendant of Sun Wu and also a military thinker, in the Warring States period. In the past some people thought the two men's works on warfare were the same book. In 1972 a Han tomb at Yinqueshan (銀雀山) in Shandong yielded both The Art of War and Sun Bin's Art of War, proving they are two different works.
How we know

Histories, bamboo slips and tombs

Histories handed down

The Spring and Autumn Annals, and the Zuo Commentary (左傳, Zuozhuan) that explains it, record the history of the Spring and Autumn period in detail; for the Warring States period we rely mainly on the Strategies of the Warring States and on the Records of the Grand Historian by Sima Qian (司馬遷) of the Han dynasty. These books are vividly written, but they also mix in a good deal of later imagination and exaggeration: many of the long speeches by persuaders, for example, were composed by later writers.

Bamboo slips from the ground

People at the time wrote on strips of bamboo, then tied them together with cord into rolls, called "slips" (簡). In recent decades large numbers of bamboo slips have been found in Warring States, Qin and Han tombs, including early versions of ancient books as well as laws and government documents. They let scholars check, and even rewrite, what the histories handed down tell us.

Archaeological digs

City walls of the states' capitals, nobles' tombs, weapons, coins and everyday objects keep coming out of the ground. The tomb of Marquis Yi of Zeng and the bone pits at Changping both let us see the life and warfare of the time directly.

Key terms

Terms from this era

Spring and Autumn / Warring States (春秋 / 戰國)
The first and second halves of the Eastern Zhou, named after two books: the Spring and Autumn Annals and the Strategies of the Warring States.
Hegemon (霸主)
In the Spring and Autumn period, the ruler of a powerful state who summoned the lords and kept order in place of the Zhou king.
Hundred Schools of Thought (百家爭鳴)
The free debate and competition between many schools of thought in the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods.
Ren (仁)
The virtue Confucius valued most: caring for others.
Dao (道)
In Daoism, the fundamental pattern by which everything in the universe works.
Reform (變法)
An overhaul of a state's whole system; the most famous is Shang Yang's reform of Qin.
County (縣)
A local unit run by an official sent directly by the ruler, replacing grants of land to nobles. China still has counties today.
Vertical and horizontal alliances (合縱連橫)
The diplomatic strategies of the six states uniting against Qin (vertical) and of Qin winning over states one at a time (horizontal).
Hong Kong

The Dragon Boat Festival in Hong Kong

Qu Yuan's story is still played out in Hong Kong today. The Dragon Boat Festival, on the fifth day of the fifth lunar month, is a public holiday in Hong Kong, where people call it the "Fifth Month Festival" (五月節). On that day there are dragon boat races in the bays of Stanley (赤柱), Tai Po (大埔), Sai Kung (西貢), Cheung Chau (長洲) and elsewhere, with crews paddling together to the beat of a drum, and families eat zongzi (糉子), glutinous rice wrapped in bamboo leaves.

In the Lantau (大嶼山) fishing village of Tai O (大澳), the Dragon Boat Water Parade (端午龍舟遊涌) has a history of more than a hundred years: fishermen row dragon boats towing small sampans carrying statues of deities through the waterways, to bless the community. It is listed as national intangible cultural heritage. Every year the Hong Kong Tourism Board also holds international dragon boat races in Victoria Harbour (維多利亞港), drawing teams from around the world, and Britain has plenty of dragon boat clubs too.

Meanwhile in Europe and the world

An age of great thinkers

Thinkers appearing at the same time

Around Confucius's lifetime, the Buddha was also teaching in India (his exact dates are still debated); about ten years after Confucius died, Socrates was born in Athens; and Mencius was almost an exact contemporary of Aristotle (see Figure 2). The German philosopher Karl Jaspers called these few centuries the "Axial Age": in several parts of the world, thinkers each independently put forward fundamental ideas that would shape the next two thousand years and more.

Athens, Alexander and Rome

While China's lords were swallowing one another up, the Greek city-states were also at war, and Athens developed democracy. From 336 to 323 BC Alexander the Great fought his way from Greece to the borders of India, but he never reached China. Three years after Qin unified China, the Carthaginian general Hannibal crossed the Alps to attack Rome.

Iron Age Scotland

In the same period Britain was in the Iron Age, and people lived in hillforts and round wooden houses. People in the north and west of Scotland began building tall round stone towers called brochs, some of which still stand today, such as the Broch of Mousa in Shetland.

Sites and museums

  • Temple, Mansion and Cemetery of Confucius (孔廟、孔府、孔林)Qufu, Shandong

    A World Heritage Site in Confucius's home town. The cemetery is where Confucius and his descendants are buried.

  • Dujiangyan (都江堰)Chengdu, Sichuan

    A World Heritage Site: a waterworks more than 2,200 years old that still irrigates the Chengdu Plain today.

  • Hubei Provincial Museum (湖北省博物館)Wuhan, Hubei

    Home to the bells of Marquis Yi of Zeng and the Sword of Goujian, with regular performances on a replica set of the bells.

  • Qi Capital Site Museum and China Ancient Chariot Museum (齊國故城遺址博物館、中國古車博物館)Linzi, Zibo, Shandong

    The site of the Qi capital, a large pit of sacrificed horses, and ancient chariots and horses.

  • Jingzhou Museum (荊州博物館)Jingzhou, Hubei

    The site of Ying, the Chu capital; the museum holds large collections of Chu silks, lacquerware and bamboo slips.

  • Confucius Hall (孔聖堂)Causeway Bay, Hong Kong

    Completed in 1935, an institution promoting Confucian learning in Hong Kong, with a secondary school attached.

  • British Museum, China galleryLondon, UK · Free
